## The running toy

Two bags of colored balls. Bag A holds 80 percent red and 20 percent
green. Bag B holds 30 percent red and 70 percent green. The prior
probability of drawing bag A is 0.6, bag B is 0.4. One draw is made
and the ball is red. The hidden bag is z in {A, B}. The observation
is x = R. Every number in this lesson was computed from these four
parameters or from seed 7 draws stated in the text. Figure scripts
print the audited values.

## C01, latent marginalization

Motivating question: if the bag is hidden, what is the probability of
the red ball we actually saw?

Start from zero. The draw comes from bag A with probability 0.6 and
bag B with probability 0.4. Bag A gives red with probability 0.8.
Bag B gives red with probability 0.3. The total probability of red is
the sum over the two hidden paths: 0.6 x 0.8 + 0.4 x 0.3 = 0.48 +
0.12 = 0.60. The hidden bag disappeared from the answer. Figure
u02_f01_latent_marginalization.png shows the two joint masses
collapsing into one marginal. That collapse is the whole move.

The idea: p(x) = sum_z p(x, z) = sum_z p(z) p(x|z). The latent z is
a nuisance the model sums away. The joint law p(x, z) holds
everything. The marginal p(x) is what the data can see. Training on
data can only ever score the marginal.

Computed example: P(R, A) = 0.48, P(R, B) = 0.12, so P(R) = 0.60.
Similarly P(G, A) = 0.6 x 0.2 = 0.12, P(G, B) = 0.4 x 0.7 = 0.28, so
P(G) = 0.40. The two marginals sum to 1.000.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: z is a discrete latent with
two states. x is the observed outcome. p(z) is unitless prior mass.
p(x|z) is the likelihood table, shape (2, 2). Assumption: the latent
set is fixed and finite here. Continuous latents integrate instead
of summing.

Derivation: the law of total probability. p(x) = sum_z p(x|z) p(z).
Each term is one hidden path. The sum is exact when the latent set
is enumerable.

Code:

    prior = np.array([0.6, 0.4])
    like_R = np.array([0.8, 0.3])
    joint_R = prior * like_R
    print(joint_R)   # [0.48 0.12]
    print(joint_R.sum())  # 0.6

Expected output: the joint masses and 0.6.

Check: joint sums to the marginal. Both marginals sum to 1.

Cost: O(K) for K latent states. Intractable when z is high
dimensional or continuous with no closed form. That cost drives the
rest of the unit.

Alternatives: keep z observed. Then no marginalization is needed and
plain maximum likelihood applies. Latent models earn their keep only
when z is genuinely hidden.

Failure case: marginalizing over the wrong latent set. If a third
bag exists and the model sums over two, the marginal misses real
paths and the likelihood lies.

Research reading: latent variable model texts (background).
Falsifiable extension: add a third bag to the toy with known
parameters. Fit the two-bag model by maximum marginal likelihood.
Measure the bias in the fitted parameters.

## C02, posterior

Motivating question: we saw red. Which bag was probably used?

Start from zero. Red came from bag A with joint mass 0.48 and from
bag B with joint mass 0.12. Renormalize by the marginal 0.60: P(A|R)
= 0.48/0.60 = 0.8, P(B|R) = 0.12/0.60 = 0.2. The observation moved
the belief from (0.6, 0.4) to (0.8, 0.2). Red is evidence for bag A
because bag A is redder.

The idea: the posterior p(z|x) is the belief about the hidden cause
after seeing the data. Prior belief times likelihood, renormalized.
It answers "what explains this observation", not "what happens
next". That distinction is the most common confusion in the unit.

Computed example: had the draw been green, P(G) = 0.40, P(A|G) =
0.12/0.40 = 0.3, P(B|G) = 0.28/0.40 = 0.7. Green is evidence for bag
B. Two draws of red: start from posterior (0.8, 0.2) as the new
prior. Joint with the second red: 0.8 x 0.8 = 0.64, 0.2 x 0.3 =
0.06. Marginal 0.70. Posterior (0.914, 0.086). Evidence accumulates.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: p(z|x) is a law over z for a
fixed x. Unitless, sums to 1 over z. Assumption: the prior and
likelihood are trusted. A wrong likelihood gives a confidently wrong
posterior.

Mechanism: divide the joint by the marginal. The joint holds
"cause and effect together". The division zooms into the slice
where x happened and rescales it to a law.

Code:

    post_R = joint_R / joint_R.sum()
    print(post_R)  # [0.8 0.2]

Check: posterior sums to 1. Posterior after data lies between prior
and certainty in the direction the likelihood favors.

Cost: one marginalization per posterior query. O(K) discrete.
Continuous posteriors need the same intractable integral as the
marginal. That is why the unit builds approximations.

Alternatives: the MAP latent, argmax_z p(z|x). A point estimate,
cheap, discards uncertainty. Use it for decisions, not for honest
beliefs.

Failure case: reading p(x|z) as the posterior. The likelihood of red
given bag A is 0.8, but that number answers a different question.
The posterior needs the prior and the marginal. Confusing the two is
the prosecutor's fallacy in miniature.

Research reading: Bayesian inference texts (background).
Falsifiable extension: none at this level. The claim is definitional.

## C03, Bayes

Motivating question: what is the general machine that turns
likelihoods into posteriors?

Start from zero. Bayes rule is the C02 computation written once for
all x: p(z|x) = p(x|z) p(z) / p(x). The numerator is the joint. The
denominator is the marginal from C01. Nothing else enters. Every
posterior in this unit is this ratio.

The idea: Bayes inverts the causal direction. The model generates
z -> x. The data arrive as x. Bayes walks backward: p(z|x) tells
which hidden causes explain the observation. The
denominator p(x) is the evidence. It normalizes and also scores the
model itself: larger evidence means a better model.

Computed example: p(A|R) = p(R|A) p(A) / p(R) = 0.8 x 0.6 / 0.60 =
0.8. The two-red sequence from C02 applied Bayes twice. After the
first red the posterior was (0.8, 0.2). After the second: (0.914,
0.086). Each new draw multiplies by its likelihood and renormalizes.
The order of the draws does not matter. Verify: R then G gives the
same posterior as G then R. Joint for (R, G): bag A path 0.6 x 0.8 x
0.2 = 0.096, bag B path 0.4 x 0.3 x 0.7 = 0.084. Posterior A =
0.096/0.180 = 0.533. Same either order.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: all three factors are laws or
law-shaped. p(x) is a positive scalar for the observed x.
Assumption: p(x) > 0. An observation outside the model support breaks
the ratio (U01-C02).

Derivation: from the product rule p(x, z) = p(x|z) p(z) = p(z|x)
p(x). Divide by p(x). One line. The depth is in what each factor
costs to compute.

Code:

    def posterior(like, prior):
        joint = like * prior
        return joint / joint.sum()
    print(posterior(np.array([0.8, 0.3]), prior))  # [0.8 0.2]

Check: output sums to 1. Swapping the draw order leaves the two-draw
posterior unchanged.

Cost: the denominator is the marginalization cost. Exact Bayes is
only as cheap as the marginal.

Alternatives: conjugate priors give closed-form posteriors. Beta
prior with Bernoulli likelihood stays Beta. Use conjugacy when the
family allows it. Most deep models do not.

Failure case: a prior that puts zero mass on the true cause. No
amount of data revives it. The posterior stays zero there forever.
Choose priors with full support.

Research reading: Bayesian data analysis texts (background).
Falsifiable extension: run the two-draw update with a prior of
(0.99, 0.01) versus (0.5, 0.5). Count how many red draws each needs
to reach posterior 0.9 on bag A. Report the asymmetry.

## C04, Jensen

Motivating question: how do we bound a log of an average, the exact
quantity inside every marginal likelihood?

Start from zero. Take the convex curve phi(t) = t^2. Average the
outputs of 1 and 3 with weights (0.7, 0.3): 0.7 x 1 + 0.3 x 9 = 3.4.
Now square the average: (0.7 x 1 + 0.3 x 3)^2 = 1.6^2 = 2.56. The
average of the curve sits above the curve of the average: 3.4 >=
2.56. Convexity bends the curve upward, so averaging first and
curving second always undershoots. Figure u02_f04 shows this with
the log curve used in the ELBO.

The idea: Jensen inequality. For convex phi, E[phi(X)] >=
phi(E[X]). For concave phi the direction flips. The logarithm is
concave, so E[log X] <= log E[X]. That flipped version is the
engine of variational inference: the log sits outside the
marginalization sum, and Jensen moves a tractable expectation
inside the log.

Computed example: the numbers above, E[phi] = 3.4, phi(E) = 2.56.
With the concave log on toy values: E_q[log v] for q = (0.7, 0.3)
and v = (0.48, 0.12) is 0.7 x log 0.48 + 0.3 x log 0.12 = -0.5138 -
0.6361 = -1.1499. Log of the mean: log(0.7 x 0.48 + 0.3 x 0.12) =
log 0.372 = -0.9889. Indeed -1.1499 <= -0.9889.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: phi is a real function on the
support of X. Convex means the chord lies above the graph.
Assumption: the expectation exists and is finite.

Derivation: a convex phi lies above each tangent line. Take
expectations of both sides at the tangent point E[X]. The linear
term vanishes and the inequality remains. The key step needs only
that one tangent bound.

Code:

    q = np.array([0.7, 0.3])
    v = np.array([1.0, 3.0])
    print((q * v**2).sum(), (q * v).sum()**2)  # 3.4 2.56

Check: the first number is never smaller. Equality holds only when
X is constant or phi is linear on the support.

Cost: O(1) per check on a toy. The value is conceptual: it justifies
the bound that the next concept builds on.

Alternatives: no alternative bounds the log-sum without extra
structure. Importance weighting and tighter multi-sample bounds
(U06 extensions) sharpen Jensen, never replace it.

Failure case: applying Jensen with the wrong curvature. Treating
log as convex flips the ELBO into an upper bound and every
"improvement" of it can silently lower the true likelihood.

Research reading: convex analysis texts (background).
Falsifiable extension: none at this level. The claim is a theorem
with stated conditions.

## C05, ELBO identity

Motivating question: the marginal log p(R) = -0.5108 needs a sum we
cannot afford in real models. What tractable object can we maximize
instead?

Start from zero. Pick any law q(z) over the hidden bag. For x = R,
define ELBO = E_q[log p(R, z) - log q(z)]. With q = (0.7, 0.3): log
p(R, z) = (log 0.48, log 0.12) = (-0.7340, -2.1203). The expectation
is 0.7 x (-0.7340) + 0.3 x (-2.1203) = -1.1499. The entropy H(q) =
0.6109. ELBO = -1.1499 + 0.6109 = -0.5390. Compare log p(R) =
-0.5108. The ELBO sits 0.0282 below. Figure u02_f02_elbo_gap.png
shows the bound and the gap as stacked bars.

The idea: ELBO = E_q[log p(x|z)] - KL(q(z)||p(z)). Reconstruction
minus regularization. The first term rewards q for putting mass
where the data are likely. The second term pulls q toward the prior.
The identity is log p(x) = ELBO + KL(q(z)||p(z|x)). Since KL >= 0,
the ELBO is a lower bound. Maximizing it over q tightens the bound
and over model parameters fits the data. One objective does both
jobs.

Computed example: the numbers above. Reconstruction: E_q[log p(R|z)]
= 0.7 x log 0.8 + 0.3 x log 0.3 = -0.1562 - 0.3612 = -0.5174.
KL(q||prior) = 0.7 x log(0.7/0.6) + 0.3 x log(0.3/0.4) = 0.1079 -
0.0863 = 0.0216. ELBO = -0.5174 - 0.0216 = -0.5390. Matches the
joint form. The decomposition is the working form in code.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: q(z) any law on the latent
set, unitless. ELBO in nats, on the same scale as log likelihood.
Assumption: q has support inside the joint support, else the KL is
infinite.

Derivation: log p(x) = log sum_z p(x,z) = log sum_z q(z) p(x,z)/q(z)
= log E_q[p(x,z)/q(z)]. Jensen with concave log: >= E_q[log
p(x,z)/q(z)] = ELBO. The gap is exactly KL(q||p(z|x)), shown in C06.

Code:

    log_joint = np.log(joint_R)
    Hq = -(q * np.log(q)).sum()
    elbo = (q * (log_joint - np.log(q))).sum()
    print(elbo)  # -0.539

Expected output: -0.5390.

Check: ELBO <= log p(x). ELBO + KL(q||posterior) equals log p(x)
within 1e-12.

Cost: O(K) per datapoint for the expectation, O(nK) per epoch.
Continuous latents need Monte Carlo inside the expectation (C09).

Alternatives: exact marginal likelihood when K is small. Laplace
approximation around the posterior mode. MCMC for asymptotically
exact samples at higher cost. ELBO wins when speed beats exactness.

Failure case: maximizing a loose ELBO. A bad q family keeps the
bound far below the likelihood. The optimizer then improves the
bound without improving the model. Watch the gap, not just the
bound.

Research reading: variational inference texts (background).
Falsifiable extension: with q fixed at (0.7, 0.3), vary the prior
over a grid and record ELBO versus true log marginal. Confirm the
bound holds at every grid point and the gap equals KL(q||posterior).

## C06, variational gap

Motivating question: the ELBO is 0.0282 below the truth. What is
that number, exactly?

Start from zero. Set q equal to the true posterior (0.8, 0.2) and
recompute the ELBO. E_q[log p(R,z)] = 0.8 x (-0.7340) + 0.2 x
(-2.1203) = -0.5872 - 0.4241 = -1.0113. H(q) = 0.5004. ELBO =
-1.0113 + 0.5004 = -0.5108. Exactly log p(R). The gap vanished.
The gap is not mysterious. It is the distance from q to the true
posterior.

The idea: the variational gap is KL(q(z)||p(z|x)). The ELBO
identity from C05 says log p(x) = ELBO + gap. Minimizing the gap
over q is the same as maximizing the ELBO. When the family contains
the posterior, the gap hits zero and the bound is tight.

Computed example: with q = (0.7, 0.3), gap = KL(q||posterior) =
0.7 x log(0.7/0.8) + 0.3 x log(0.3/0.2) = -0.0935 + 0.1216 =
0.0282. With q = posterior, gap = 0. With q = prior (0.6, 0.4), gap
= 0.6 x log(0.6/0.8) + 0.4 x log(0.4/0.2) = -0.1726 + 0.2773 =
0.1047. The prior is the worst q of the three.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: the gap is in nats, >= 0.
Assumption: both q and the posterior are laws on the same latent
set.

Derivation: expand KL(q||p(z|x)) = E_q[log q - log p(z|x)]. Write
p(z|x) = p(x,z)/p(x). The log p(x) term is constant in z and pulls
out. What remains is -ELBO + log p(x). Rearranged: log p(x) = ELBO
+ KL. The derivation is the same algebra as C05, read backward.

Code:

    gap = (q * np.log(q / post_R)).sum()
    print(gap, logp - elbo)  # 0.0282 0.0282

Check: the two computations agree within 1e-12. The gap is zero
exactly when q equals the posterior.

Cost: the gap costs one KL evaluation. O(K) discrete. The catch:
it needs the true posterior, which is what we wanted to avoid.
In practice the gap is unobservable. We watch the ELBO and bound
the gap by family design instead.

Alternatives: tighter bounds (importance-weighted ELBO) shrink the
effective gap with more samples. Same family, better estimator.

Failure case: a degenerate q that puts all mass on one z. The gap
becomes -log p(z|x) at that point. If the chosen z is wrong, the
ELBO collapses and the model trains on a fiction.

Research reading: variational inference texts on bound tightness
(background). Falsifiable extension: on the toy, grid over q in
steps of 0.05 and plot ELBO versus gap. Confirm the sum is constant
at log p(R) = -0.5108 everywhere.

## C07, reverse and forward KL

Motivating question: which fit does the optimizer prefer, the one
that covers everything or the one that picks a mode?

Start from zero. The true posterior over a continuous latent is
two-moded: half the mass near -3, half near 3. Two candidate
Gaussians: a cover q = N(0, 2.2^2) that spreads over both modes, and
a seek q = N(3, 1) that sits on the right mode. Under forward KL,
KL(p||q), the cover wins: 0.632 versus 8.311 nats. Under reverse
KL, KL(q||p), the seek wins: 0.689 versus 1.007 nats. Same two
candidates, opposite winners. Figure u02_f03_forward_reverse_kl.png
shows the truth and both fits with the verdicts.

The idea: forward KL = E_p[log p/q] averages over the truth. Where
p has mass and q has none, the penalty explodes. So q must cover
every mode: mean-seeking, zero-avoiding. Reverse KL = E_q[log q/p]
averages over the fit. Where q has mass and p has none, the
penalty explodes. So q must sit where p is large: mode-seeking,
zero-forcing. Variational inference minimizes reverse KL. It picks
modes. Maximum likelihood minimizes forward KL. It covers modes.

Computed example: the quadrature numbers above, computed on a grid
of 4001 points over [-8, 8]. The forward loss of the seek fit is
8.311 nats because the left mode at -3 sits far in its tail. The
reverse loss of the cover fit is 1.007 nats because the cover puts
mass near 0 where the truth has a valley.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: both KLs in nats. p and q are
densities on the line. Assumption: quadrature resolves the tails.
Grid checks in the script confirm stability.

Mechanism: the direction of the expectation decides which law is
allowed to have zeros. E_p punishes q zeros. E_q punishes p zeros
under q mass. Everything else follows.

Code:

    # quadrature sketch: p_mix, q_cover, q_seek on grid xs
    dx = xs[1] - xs[0]
    fkl = (p_mix * np.log(p_mix / q_cover))[p_mix > 0].sum() * dx
    print(fkl)  # about 0.632

Check: both KLs are >= 0. Swapping p and q changes the value.

Cost: quadrature O(grid). The lesson is conceptual: know which
direction your loss uses.

Alternatives: symmetric divergences (Jensen-Shannon) split the
difference. Alpha-divergences interpolate the two behaviors with a
knob. Use them when neither pure behavior fits the task.

Failure case: training a VAE (reverse KL) and expecting diverse
samples. The objective rewards mode-seeking. Blurry or dropped
modes are the objective working as designed, not a bug to patch
with more capacity.

Research reading: divergence texts on variational objectives
(background). Falsifiable extension: fit one Gaussian to the
two-mode toy by minimizing forward versus reverse KL with a fixed
optimizer budget. Record which fit covers both modes. The
prediction: forward covers, reverse picks one.

## C08, reparameterization

Motivating question: the ELBO has an expectation over q, and q has
parameters. How does a gradient flow through a random sample?

Start from zero. Let q(z) = N(mu, sigma^2) and f(z) = z^2. The
objective E_q[f] = mu^2 + sigma^2. True gradients: d/dmu = 2 mu =
2.0, d/dsigma = 2 sigma = 4.0 at mu = 1, sigma = 2. The naive
gradient estimator draws z and uses the score identity: E[f(z)
d/dmu log q(z)]. It is unbiased but noisy: over 50 repeats with
1000 samples each, seed 7, the estimates have standard deviation
0.2546. The reparameterization trick rewrites z = mu + sigma x
eps with eps ~ N(0,1) fixed. Now E_q[f] = E_eps[(mu + sigma x
eps)^2] and the gradient moves inside: d/dmu E = E_eps[2(mu +
sigma x eps)]. Same 1000 samples, standard deviation 0.1171. Less
than half the noise. Figure u02_f04_reparam_variance.png shows the
two histograms.

The idea: separate the randomness from the parameters. The noise
eps carries no parameters. The deterministic map g(mu, sigma, eps)
carries them all. Gradients then flow through g by the chain rule,
and the Monte Carlo average has far lower variance because the
sampling distribution no longer moves with the parameters.

Computed example: the numbers above. Reparam mean 1.9781 versus true
2.0, std 0.1171. Score-function mean 1.9165, std 0.2546. Both are
unbiased. One is usable in a minibatch. The other is not.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: eps has the shape of z and a
fixed law. g must be differentiable in the parameters. Assumption:
q is reparameterizable: location-scale families, and others via
implicit gradients, qualify. Discrete latents do not (U03-C09).

Derivation: E_{z~q_theta}[f(z)] = E_{eps}[f(g(theta, eps))].
Differentiate under the integral: d/dtheta E = E_eps[df/dg x dg/d
theta]. The interchange needs mild regularity, the standard
dominated-convergence condition taught in P05.

Code:

    eps = rng.standard_normal(1000)
    z = mu + sig * eps
    grad_mu = (2.0 * z).mean()
    print(grad_mu)  # near 2.0

Expected output: about 1.98 with seed 7.

Check: compare against the closed form 2 mu. Finite-difference the
ELBO in mu as an independent check.

Cost: O(K) per gradient, same as the score function. The win is
statistical, not computational: fewer samples per usable step.

Alternatives: the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator works for any
q, including discrete. Control variates and baselines cut its
variance. Use it where reparameterization is impossible.

Failure case: applying the trick to a discrete latent by
differentiating through an argmax. The gradient is zero almost
everywhere and the parameters never move. The fix is a continuous
relaxation or the score function.

Research reading: variational autoencoder texts on gradient
estimators (background). Falsifiable extension: repeat the
variance comparison for f(z) = z^4 at mu = 0, sigma = 1. Predict
which estimator degrades faster and measure the std ratio.

## C09, Monte Carlo variance

Motivating question: how many samples does the gradient estimate
need before it is trustworthy?

Start from zero. The reparameterized gradient estimate is an
average over K draws. Its standard error shrinks like 1/sqrt(K).
Measured on the toy with seed 7, 40 repeats each: K = 100 gives std
0.3956, K = 400 gives 0.2041, K = 1600 gives 0.1001. Quadrupling K
halves the noise. The ratios: 0.3956/0.2041 = 1.94, 0.2041/0.1001
= 2.04. The 1/sqrt(K) law holds on the nose.

The idea: Monte Carlo error is O(1/sqrt(K)) regardless of
dimension. That is both the promise and the curse: the rate never
improves with dimension, and it never degrades either. The constant
depends on the integrand variance. Reparameterization shrinks the
constant. More samples shrink the rate term. Budget goes to
whichever is cheaper.

Computed example: the three std values above. To reach std 0.05 on
this toy needs about K = 6400 by the 1/sqrt law: 0.1001 x
sqrt(1600/6400) = 0.0501. That is the planning formula. Measure at
two K values, extrapolate, then verify at the third.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: K is the sample count per
gradient step. Std is in gradient units. Assumption: draws are IID.
Correlated draws (MCMC) need effective-sample-size corrections.

Mechanism: variance of a mean is variance/K. The square root gives
the std law. No assumptions about the integrand shape beyond finite
variance.

Code:

    ests = [(2.0 * (mu + sig * rng.standard_normal(K))).mean()
            for _ in range(40)]
    print(np.std(ests))  # about 0.40 at K=100

Check: the std ratio between K and 4K is near 2. A ratio far from
2 signals correlated draws or a bug.

Cost: O(K) compute per step. Memory O(K) if samples are stored,
O(1) streaming. The real cost is wall time per unit of variance
reduction.

Alternatives: quasi-Monte Carlo and control variates reduce the
constant. Analytic expectations remove sampling where closed forms
exist (Gaussian KL in U03-C04). Use the cheapest variance reduction
available before raising K.

Failure case: a single-sample gradient (K = 1) with a high-variance
integrand. The optimizer random-walks. Loss curves look like noise
because they are noise. Diagnose by fixing the seed and re-running:
identical seeds give identical curves only if the variance comes
from sampling, not from a bug.

Research reading: Monte Carlo method texts (background).
Falsifiable extension: predict the K needed for std 0.05 from the
K = 100 and K = 400 measurements, then measure at that K and report
the prediction error.

## C10, amortization

Motivating question: with 10,000 data points, do we really optimize
a separate q for each one?

Start from zero. Per-point variational inference learns q_i(z) for
each x_i. With latent dimension d = 2, each q_i needs a mean and a
log-variance: 4 numbers. For n = 100 points that is 400 parameters.
For n = 10,000 it is 40,000. Amortized inference learns one encoder
network e(x) that outputs the 4 numbers for any x. A small net from
R^8 through 32 hidden units to 4 outputs has 8 x 32 + 32 + 32 x 4 +
4 = 420 parameters. Constant in n. At n = 100 the encoder is about
tied. At n = 10,000 it uses 95 times fewer parameters, and a new
point needs one forward pass instead of a fresh optimization.

The idea: amortization trades per-point optimization for one shared
function approximator. The encoder learns the mapping "data point
-> posterior parameters" across the dataset. Inference on a new
point becomes a forward pass. The price is the amortization gap:
the encoder may not reach the per-point optimum, especially on
unusual points.

Computed example: the parameter counts above. Crossover: per-point
4n versus 420. The encoder wins on count for n > 105. On top of
count, per-point inference at test time costs an optimization loop
per point while amortized costs one pass.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: n data points, d latent
dims, encoder params P_enc fixed. Assumption: the posterior mapping
is learnable by the chosen architecture. A too-small encoder
underfits every point at once.

Mechanism: the ELBO summed over points becomes a function of the
encoder weights. One gradient step updates the encoder on a
minibatch and improves q for all points at once. Information is
shared across the dataset through the weights.

Code:

    per_point = n * d * 2
    amortized = 8 * 32 + 32 + 32 * (d * 2) + d * 2
    print(per_point, amortized)  # 400 420 at n=100

Check: at n = 10,000 the ratio is 40,000/420 = 95.2.

Cost: training the encoder costs one network training run.
Inference per point is O(P_enc) forward. Per-point VI costs an
optimization per point forever.

Alternatives: per-point VI for small n or when the encoder
underfits. Hybrid: amortized init plus a few per-point refinement
steps. Use the hybrid when test points look nothing like training.

Failure case: the amortization gap on outliers. The encoder was
trained on typical points. A weird test point gets a bad q, a
loose bound, and a silently wrong posterior. Detect it by comparing
the encoder ELBO against a few per-point refinement steps on held
out points.

Research reading: variational autoencoder texts on inference
networks (background). Falsifiable extension: none at this level.
The claim is architectural arithmetic.

## C11, expressive families

Motivating question: what if the true posterior is correlated and q
is a diagonal Gaussian?

Start from zero. The true posterior is N(0, Sigma) with Sigma =
[[1, 0.9], [0.9, 1]], strongly correlated. The variational family
is mean-field: diagonal Gaussians, no correlation allowed. The best
member of the family is N(0, I). The irreducible gap is KL(N(0,
Sigma)||N(0, I)) = 0.5 x (tr(Sigma) - 2 + log(1/det Sigma)) =
0.5 x (2 - 2 + log(1/0.19)) = 0.8304 nats. No member of the family
can do better. The bound is loose by at least 0.8304 nats forever,
no matter how long training runs.

The idea: the variational family is the set q is allowed to be.
Mean-field factorizes across dimensions. Full-covariance Gaussians
allow correlation. Normalizing flows (U04) allow non-Gaussian
shapes. A richer family shrinks the gap. A poorer family leaves an
irreducible gap that no optimizer can close. Family choice is a
modeling decision with a permanent cost.

Computed example: the 0.8304 nat gap above. If the family is
upgraded to full-covariance Gaussians, the best member matches
Sigma exactly and the gap from this source drops to 0. The upgrade
costs d(d+1)/2 covariance parameters instead of d.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: Sigma is 2x2 positive
definite. Gap in nats. Assumption: the rest of the model is fixed.
In practice family and model interact.

Derivation: the Gaussian KL closed form. KL(N0||N1) = 0.5 x
(tr(Sigma1^-1 Sigma0) + (mu1-mu0)^T Sigma1^-1 (mu1-mu0) - k +
log(det Sigma1 / det Sigma0)). Plug Sigma1 = I, mu = 0.

Code:

    S0 = np.array([[1.0, 0.9], [0.9, 1.0]])
    gap = 0.5 * (np.trace(S0) - 2 + np.log(1/np.linalg.det(S0)))
    print(gap)  # 0.8304

Check: gap >= 0. Upgrading the family can only lower it.

Cost: mean-field O(d) params. Full covariance O(d^2). Flows O(d)
per layer with several layers. The statistical price of richness
is harder optimization and noisier gradients.

Alternatives: structured families (low-rank plus diagonal) split
the difference. MCMC skips the family question at sampling cost.
Choose by the posterior shape the data actually produce.

Failure case: a mean-field q on a posterior with two correlated
modes. It centers between the modes or picks one (C07). Either way
the uncertainty report is wrong. Diagnose with posterior
predictive checks on held-out data.

Research reading: variational family design texts (background).
Falsifiable extension: fit mean-field and full-covariance q to the
correlated toy with matched optimizer budgets. Confirm the ELBO
difference is near 0.8304 nats.

## C12, degeneracy

Motivating question: two different latent settings give the same
marginal. Can the data tell them apart?

Start from zero. Swap the bag labels: prior (0.4, 0.6) with
likelihoods swapped, P(R|A') = 0.3, P(R|B') = 0.8. The marginal:
0.4 x 0.3 + 0.6 x 0.8 = 0.12 + 0.48 = 0.60. Identical to the
original 0.60. The data see the same number. The parameters moved
and nothing observable changed. That is label-switching degeneracy,
the latent twin of U01-C12.

The idea: latent models have symmetries. Permuting latent labels
with matching parameter permutations leaves every marginal
unchanged. The likelihood has one ridge per symmetry. The posterior
over parameters is multimodal with identical modes. Point
estimates land on an arbitrary mode. Parameter averages across
modes are meaningless. Only label-invariant quantities (the
marginal, predictive densities, partitions up to permutation) are
identified.

Computed example: the swapped toy above. Also the posterior over z
given R under the swapped labeling is (0.2, 0.8): the mirror image
of (0.8, 0.2). Every observable matches. Every label disagrees.

Variables, units, shapes, assumptions: the symmetry group acts on
the latent indices. Assumption: the symmetry is exact. Approximate
symmetries give near-ridges that slow optimizers the same way.

Mechanism: p(x) = sum_z p(x|z) p(z). A permutation pi gives sum_z
p(x|pi(z)) p(pi(z)). Relabel the sum index. The terms are the same
set. The sum is identical.

Code:

    prior_s = np.array([0.4, 0.6])
    like_s = np.array([0.3, 0.8])
    print((prior_s * like_s).sum())  # 0.6

Check: the marginal matches the original to 1e-12. The posterior
is the permuted mirror.

Cost: conceptual. The practical cost is wasted compute exploring
equivalent modes and misleading parameter reports.

Alternatives: break symmetry with ordered priors or canonical
parameterizations. Or accept it and report only invariant
quantities. The VAE community mostly accepts it.

Failure case: averaging the latent means of a mixture across
training runs with different labelings. The average sits between
real clusters and describes nothing. Always align labels before
comparing, or compare invariant statistics.

Research reading: identifiability in latent variable texts
(background). Falsifiable extension: fit the two-bag model twice
from different seeds on fixed toy data. Measure label agreement
after optimal permutation. Low agreement confirms the symmetry
empirically.
