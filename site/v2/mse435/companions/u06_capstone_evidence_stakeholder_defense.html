Section 1 · C01

The baseline: measure first, propose second

From the lessonC01 §5

The baseline workflow: 200 cases per day, 45 minutes each, 38 dollars per case, 250 days per year. Yearly cost is 200 times 38 times 250, or 1,900,000 dollars. Yearly hours: 37,500. Error rate 0.04: 2,000 errors per year at 120 dollars each, or 240,000. True baseline: 2.14M per year.

Measure first, propose second.

From the lessonC01 §3 Direct quote from the lesson page.

From the lessonC01 §6

The AI proposal claims 30 percent cost reduction. Against the 2.14M baseline that is 642,000 per year. Against an unmeasured 1.5M guess it would be 450,000. The baseline choice moves the claim by 192,000.

Nugget 1 · From the lessonC01 §6

The baseline moves the claim by 192,000 dollars. A 30 percent saving is worth 642,000 against the measured 2.14M baseline and 450,000 against an unmeasured 1.5M guess. The baseline choice moves the claim by 192,000.

Why it matters companion
Without a measured before, every saving is fiction. You cannot show improvement against a number you never measured.
Transfer companion
Measure first, propose second. Photograph the before state or the after means nothing.

Section 3 · C03

Measurable outcomes: primary plus guardrails

From the lessonC03 §5

Baseline: cost per case 38, resolution 0.85, CSAT 4.1. Targets: cost per case at or below 30, resolution at or above 0.85 as guardrail, CSAT at or above 4.0 as guardrail. The primary is cost. The guardrails stop the team from hitting cost by wrecking quality.

From the lessonC03 §6

After 90 days: cost per case 28, resolution 0.87, CSAT 4.2. All targets met: success. If cost is 28 but resolution is 0.79, the case fails despite the saving: the guardrail caught quality erosion.

Nugget 2 · From the lessonC03 §6

Guardrails stop you from winning wrong. Cost per case hit 28 against a 30 target, but resolution fell to 0.79 against the 0.85 guardrail. The case fails despite the saving. The guardrail caught quality erosion.

Why it matters companion
A single metric optimizes itself by breaking the others. Cost falls fastest when quality is sacrificed.
Transfer companion
Pair every primary metric with guardrails. Name what must not move before you celebrate what moved.

Section 4 · C04

The quality gate: it earns its keep by saying no

From the lessonC04 §5

Accuracy target 0.90, latency target 2.0 seconds, escalation target 0.10. Measured: 0.91, 1.8, 0.08. All pass. If latency measured 2.3: fail, no ship. The value of the gate is the failures it causes: each blocked rollout is a prevented incident.

From the lessonC04 §6

The team wants to ship at 0.89 accuracy, close enough. The gate says no. Cost of waiting one sprint: 40,000. Expected cost of shipping a miss: 0.02 extra error times 50,000 cases times 120, or 120,000. The gate saves 80,000 by saying no.

Section 5 · C05

Uncertain forecasts: survive the low case

From the lessonC05 §5

Cases: 200,000 at weight 0.25, 450,000 at 0.50, 800,000 at 0.25. Expected value: 50,000 plus 225,000 plus 200,000, or 475,000. The base case of 450,000 understates the expected value because the high case is fat.

From the lessonC05 §6

Now the low case is minus 100,000: expected value is minus 25,000 plus 225,000 plus 200,000, or 400,000, still positive, but the downside is a loss. The stakeholder question changes from whether the expected value is positive to whether the firm can survive the low case.

The downside question beats the expected-value question. Expected value is 400,000 even with a losing low case. The stakeholder question changes from whether the EV is positive to whether the firm survives the low case.

Why it matters companion
Expected value averages the loss away. Survival does not average. One ruinous case kills the project that the average approved.
Transfer companion
Show the minimum beside the mean. If the low case kills you, the expected value is decoration.

Section 6 · C06

Sensitivity: re-run the tornado

From the lessonC06 §5

Base case 768,000. Drivers: adoption 400,000 to 900,000, swing 500,000. Labor cost 600,000 to 850,000, swing 250,000. Volume 650,000 to 830,000, swing 180,000. Quality 700,000 to 820,000, swing 120,000. Ranking: adoption, labor, volume, quality. The case lives or dies on adoption.

From the lessonC06 §6

The pilot measures adoption precisely, narrowing its range to 700,000 to 850,000, swing 150,000. New ranking: labor first at 250,000. The tornado updates as ignorance shrinks: re-run it after every de-risking step.

Section 7 · C07

Total cost: the build is the tip

From the lessonC07 §5

Build 400,000. Run 300,000 per year. Labor 150,000 per year. Kill and decommission 100,000. Three-year TCO: 400,000 plus 3 times 450,000 plus 100,000, or 1,850,000. The build is 22 percent of the total. A case that priced 400,000 understated cost by 4.6x.

From the lessonC07 §6

Against the C02 PV of 768,000 in savings, the 1,850,000 TCO kills the case: net minus 1,082,000. With the trained ramp at 903,000 PV it still fails. The honest conclusion: this design is too expensive for this saving. Redesign for lower run cost or find a bigger saving.

Nugget 4 · From the lessonC07 §5, §6

The build is 22 percent of the total. A case that priced 400,000 understated cost by 4.6x. The 1,850,000 TCO kills the 768,000 saving. The honest conclusion: this design is too expensive for this saving.

Why it matters companion
Build cost is the visible tip. Run, labor, and decommission are the iceberg. Cases die on the total, not the tip.
Transfer companion
Always present TCO beside the build quote. A ratio that shocks you shocks the approver too.

Section 8 · C08

Governance: price the board against the risk

From the lessonC08 §5

A change board: 2 days delay at 10,000 per day is 20,000 per change. Incident risk: 0.05 times 200,000 is 10,000 per change. The board costs double the risk it prevents: lighten it. Weekly cadence costs 10,000 per change and breaks even. For a clinical model with 5M impact, risk is 250,000 and the board is cheap.

From the lessonC08 §6

50 changes per year. Heavy board: 1M per year in delay. Light board: 500,000. No board: 500,000 expected incidents. The light board ties no-board on cost and keeps the review: choose light.

Section 9 · C09

Rollout and rollback: the 100x discount

From the lessonC09 §5

Baseline error 0.04, trigger at 0.08. The canary runs at 0.09 for 90 minutes: above the trigger, sustained. Roll back. Damage at 1 percent traffic: 0.09 times 0.01 times 50,000 cases times 120, or 5,400. At 100 percent without stages: 540,000. Staging cut the damage 100x.

From the lessonC09 §6

The 10 percent stage runs clean for a week at error 0.035: the entry gate to 100 percent passes. Week 2 at full traffic shows error 0.06: below the 0.08 trigger but above baseline. Action: investigate, do not roll back. The trigger needs both a level and a duration.

Canary1 percent0.09 for 90 minRoll back

Nugget 5 · From the lessonC09 §5, §6

Staging cuts the damage 100x. The same bad release costs 5,400 dollars at 1 percent traffic and 540,000 at full traffic. The canary bought a 100x discount on the mistake.

Why it matters companion
Errors scale with exposure. Staged rollout buys information cheaply: each stage prices the next stage risk.
Transfer companion
Never ship to 100 percent first. The trigger needs both a level and a duration.

Section 10 · C10

Owner and handoff: the 12-item checklist

From the lessonC10 §5

On-call staffing: 2,000 per week, or 104,000 per year. Handoff value: incidents drop from 4 hours to 1 hour at 500 per hour, or 1,500 per incident. At 2 incidents per month: 36,000 per year of value against 104,000 cost. The on-call looks expensive until the unowned incident costs 50,000 in SLA credits.

From the lessonC10 §6

The handoff checklist has 12 items: runbook, dashboards, alerts, escalation, rollback button, contacts, SLOs, data retention, access, cost alerts, incident template, joint drill. Each missing item adds 30 minutes to the first real incident. Six missing items: 3 extra hours times 500, or 1,500 on the first incident alone.

Section 11 · C11

Alternatives: score the status quo

From the lessonC11 §5

Weights: cost 0.6, control 0.4. Build: 0.6 times 7 plus 0.4 times 9, or 7.8. Vendor: 0.6 times 9 plus 0.4 times 5, or 7.4. Hire: 0.6 times 5 plus 0.4 times 7, or 5.8. Build wins, but only by 0.4 over vendor: within scoring noise. The honest read is that build and vendor tie, and the decision falls to risk.

From the lessonC11 §6

Add the status quo at cost 10 and control 10: 0.6 times 10 plus 0.4 times 10, or 10. The status quo wins the matrix. The case must then argue the hidden 2.14M baseline cost, or admit the project is not worth doing.

Section 12 · C12

The falsifiable investment decision

From the lessonC12 §5

The decision rule is written before the pilot: invest X = 400,000 if the pilot shows cost reduction Y = 15 percent over D = 4 weeks at confidence C = 90 percent. Pilot result: 18 percent plus or minus 5. The 90 percent interval is 13 to 23. Lower bound 13 is below 15: the rule says kill, or extend. The interval, not the point, decides.

The rule fired on evidence, not enthusiasm.

From the lessonC12 §6 Direct quote from the lesson page.

From the lessonC12 §6

Second pilot: 22 percent plus or minus 4. Interval 18 to 26. Lower bound above 15: invest. If the pilot runs 6 weeks instead of 4 to get a better number, the rule was broken: D is part of the rule.
