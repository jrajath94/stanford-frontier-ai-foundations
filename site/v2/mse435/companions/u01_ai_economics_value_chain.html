Section 1 · C01

Supply meets demand at one point

From the lessonC01 §2

The lesson opens with a market for GPU-hours. Demand is Qd = 100 minus 2P. Supply is Qs = 20 plus 3P. P is dollars per GPU-hour. Q is GPU-hours per day.

From the lessonC01 §5

The market rests where the two lists agree. Set Qd equal to Qs and solve for P. The algebra gives P = (a minus c) divided by (b plus d). With a = 100, b = 2, c = 20, d = 3, the price is 16 dollars. Quantity is 68 GPU-hours per day.

From the lessonC01 §6

Demand then shifts. Buyers now want Qd = 140 minus 2P at every price. The new price is 24 dollars. Quantity rises to 92. The toy answers the motivating question: when buyers want more at every price, the price rises, here by 8 dollars, and quantity rises by 24.

Nugget 1 · From the lessonC01 §5, §6

The price is the meeting point. One equation locates the whole market. P = (a minus c) divided by (b plus d) turns two curves into one number: 16 dollars in the toy.

Why it matters companion
Direction comes free. A demand shift moves the point along the supply curve, so you predict the sign of the price move before you argue about its size.
Transfer companion
Find the clearing variable first. In any market, model, or queue, locate the point where the two sides agree before you debate the levels.

Section 2 · C02

Complements move together, substitutes replace

From the lessonC02 §2

GPU rental price falls 20 percent. Power demand rises 6 percent. Cross-price elasticity is the ratio: 0.06 divided by minus 0.20, which is minus 0.30. The negative sign means power use rises as GPU price falls. The two are used together. GPUs and power are complements.

From the lessonC02 §6

The substitute toy flips the sign. API token price falls 20 percent. Self-hosted GPU demand falls 10 percent. Elasticity is minus 0.10 divided by minus 0.20, which is plus 0.50. Cheaper API tokens replace self-hosting. A positive sign means substitutes.

Nugget 2 · From the lessonC02 §6

The sign carries the economics. Two toys, one ratio, opposite signs. Minus 0.30 says GPUs and power are complements. Plus 0.50 says API tokens and self-hosting are substitutes.

Why it matters companion
The classification changes the strategy. Complements get bundled and co-priced. Substitutes get defended against.
Transfer companion
Watch the cross effect. When a neighbor price moves your demand, the sign of the move names the relationship.

Section 3 · C03

Fixed cost, variable cost, and the break-even

From the lessonC03 §2

A token API charges 15 dollars per 1M-token block. Fixed cost is 100,000 dollars per month. Variable cost is 5 dollars per block. Profit is (P minus v) times Q minus FC. The term (P minus v) is the contribution margin: 10 dollars per block here.

From the lessonC03 §6

Break-even is FC divided by (P minus v): 100,000 divided by 10 is 10,000 blocks per month. At 10,000 blocks, average total cost is 15 dollars, equal to the price. At 50,000 blocks, it is 7 dollars. Same firm, same costs, half the unit cost at 5x volume.

Nugget 3 · From the lessonC03 §6

Volume is the lever. Average cost falls from 15 to 7 dollars as volume rises from 10,000 to 50,000 blocks. The fixed cost spreads over more units. The variable cost stays at 5.

Why it matters companion
Price wars are volume wars. The firm that scales first can price at a level the smaller rival cannot match.
Transfer companion
Split fixed from variable before any pricing decision. If the cost does not fall with volume, the label is wrong.

Section 4 · C04

Buy the server or rent the compute

From the lessonC04 §2

Buy a GPU server for 200,000 dollars now, or rent the same compute at 60,000 dollars per year for four years. The discount rate is 10 percent. A dollar next year is worth 1 divided by 1.10 today.

From the lessonC04 §6

The present value of four years of rent is 60,000 times 3.1699, or 190,192 dollars. Buying costs 200,000. Rent wins by 9,808 dollars before maintenance. Add 5,000 dollars per year of maintenance to the buy side: 215,849 against 190,192. Rent wins by 25,657 dollars.

Nugget 4 · From the lessonC04 §6

The discount rate is a vote about the future. Rent wins by 9,808 dollars on bare hardware, and by 25,657 once maintenance enters. The buy side looked cheaper per year until the future payments were discounted.

Why it matters companion
Capex versus opex is never about sticker prices. It is about when the money leaves.
Transfer companion
Always discount before you compare. Undiscounted totals flatter the option that spends late.

Section 5 · C05

Who keeps the value: capture along the chain

From the lessonC05 §2

An app sells 100 dollars of value. It pays 20 for the model API, 10 for cloud, 5 for chips. Each layer keeps a margin on what it passes up. Total created value is 100 minus true input costs of 27, which is 73 dollars.

From the lessonC05 §6

The toy walks the chain backward. Chips: cost 5, price 11, margin 54.5 percent. Cloud: cost 15, price 21.4, margin 29.9 percent. Model: cost 29.4, price 45.2, margin 35.0 percent. App: cost 55.2, price 100, margin 44.8 percent. Captured shares of the 73: chips 6, cloud 6.4, model 15.8, app 44.8.

Nugget 5 · From the lessonC05 §6

Bottlenecks capture more. Commodities capture less. The app keeps 44.8 of 73 dollars of created value. The chip keeps 6. The layer the buyer cannot route around takes the largest slice.

Why it matters companion
Value creation is the size of the pie. Value capture is who keeps it. The two are different games with different winners.
Transfer companion
Price the layer you cannot bypass. In any chain, the scarce interface sets the margin.

Section 6 · C06

Four layers from chips to applications

From the lessonC06 §3

One token travels from a GPU to a chat answer. Four owners take a handoff. Chips sell raw compute. Clouds sell reliable compute at scale: power, cooling, networking, uptime. Model labs sell intelligence. Apps sell outcomes.

From the lessonC06 §6

One chat answer costs the app 0.02 dollars in tokens. The stack: 0.008 model, 0.010 facility, 0.002 chip amortization. A 50 percent chip price cut saves 0.001 per answer, only 5 percent. Cost cuts at the bottom dilute as they rise.

Section 7 · C07

Consumer motion versus enterprise motion

From the lessonC07 §3

Consumers decide alone, pay little, and leave fast. Enterprises decide by committee, pay a lot, and stay for years. The unit economics differ because acquisition cost, churn, and willingness to pay differ.

From the lessonC07 §6

The toys: a consumer app with 10M free users, 2 percent paying 20 dollars a month, earns 4.0M dollars per month on 2.5M of cost, for 1.5M of profit. An enterprise product with 50 contracts at 200k dollars a year earns 10M per year on 6M of cost, for 4M per year, or 0.33M per month. One enterprise account equals 833 consumer-years of revenue.

Section 8 · C08

Capability is not productivity

From the lessonC08 §3

A tool saves 30 percent of time for its users. Only 20 percent of workers use it. Realized gain is far below 30 percent. Announcements measure capability. Payrolls measure productivity. The gap between them is adoption.

From the lessonC08 §6

The lesson models realized gain as g_max times a divided by (a plus k), with k = 0.25. At 20 percent adoption the gain is 13.3 percent. At 70 percent it is 22.1 percent. Tripling adoption raises the gain from 13.3 to 22.1, not to 30. The friction constant eats the rest.

Nugget 6 · From the lessonC08 §6

Multiply by adoption before you quote a gain. The tool promises 30 percent. At 20 percent adoption the firm gets 13.3. The headline number is capability. The realized number is capability times adoption, minus friction.

Why it matters companion
Boards fund the headline and audit the realized. The gap between them is where AI business cases die.
Transfer companion
Never present a per-user gain as a firm gain. Ask who uses it, then multiply.

Section 9 · C09

Score the forecast: bias versus noise

From the lessonC09 §3

A forecast is a bet about the future. An observation is what happened. Error is the gap. Bias is error with a direction. Noise is error without one.

From the lessonC09 §6

Toy one: observed token price 4 dollars per 1M. Forecasts 2, 3, 7. Errors minus 2, minus 1, plus 3. Mean error 0. MSE 4.67. The forecaster is unbiased but noisy. Toy two: forecasts 5, 6, 7. Bias plus 2, same MSE 4.67. The second forecaster is systematically high and can be fixed by subtracting 2.

Section 10 · C10

Hold the ruler still: the two-year comparison

From the lessonC10 §2

2024 reports price per 1M tokens at 10 dollars. 2026 reports a blended price of 4 dollars. The naive fall is 60 percent. But the ruler moved: the 2026 basket mixes premium and standard tokens.

From the lessonC10 §6

Matched comparison: premium tokens fell from 10 to 6 dollars, a 40 percent fall. Mix shift adds 20 points. The honest headline is that the premium price fell 40 percent and mix shift added 20 points.

Section 11 · C11

Replace the point forecast with a range

From the lessonC11 §2

Token demand next year: 0.5B with probability 0.25, 1B with 0.50, 2B with 0.25. Expected value is 1.125B tokens per day. The modal forecast says about 1B. The expected value exceeds it because the upside outweighs the downside.

Decisions need the range because capacity, contracts, and cash all break at the edges, not at the middle.

From the lessonC11 §3 Direct quote from the lesson page.

From the lessonC11 §6

Capacity costs 1M dollars per 1B tokens per day. Build for the 1B point forecast: expected cost 1.875M, counting idle capacity and shortfall. Build 1.5B: expected cost 2.375M. The point forecast wins here, but only the range lets you do the math.
