Section 1 · C01

Two risks: what you measure and what you pay

From the lessonC01

Empirical risk is the average loss on the sample. Population risk is the expected loss over the true distribution. The computed example, 0-1 loss: R_hat(h) = 0.25. Now fix the population: x uniform on [0, 4], y = 1 when x ≥ 1.5 else 0. The rule h is wrong exactly on x in [1.5, 2.5), a set of measure 1 out of 4. R(h) = 0.25. Here the two agree. They agree by luck of the toy, not by law. C07 breaks the luck.

Nugget 01 · Agreement by luck is not a law

Empirical risk 0.25, population risk 0.25. Same number, no guarantee. The toy got lucky. The theory does not promise it.

What happened
The lesson computes both risks at 0.25 on a uniform [0, 4] toy, then states plainly that they need not agree.
Why it matters
Chasing training error assumes the agreement. Generalization is the study of when the assumption holds.
Transferable principle
companion Never read a training number as a deployment promise. Ask what bridges the two, or admit there is none.

Section 3 · C03

Assumptions: what consistency stands on

From the lessonC03

The computed example: p = 0.7, n = 4. IID MSE = 0.0525. Dependent MSE = 0.21. Ratio 4.0, exactly the information loss. Dependence does not just add noise. It divides the effective sample size. Four dependent draws carry the information of one.

Section 4 · C04

Bias and variance: the split that adds up

From the lessonC04

k = 3, n = 4, p = 0.7. MLE k/n: bias 0, variance 0.05250, MSE 0.05250. Laplace (k+1)/(n+2): bias -0.06667, variance 0.02333, MSE 0.02778. The check: bias² + var = 0.00444 + 0.02333 = 0.02778. The split adds up. The biased estimator wins on MSE: a little bias buys a lot of variance.

From the lessonC04

Polynomial fits of degree 1 to 8 on a noisy quadratic, 100 resamples, seed 7. Bias squared falls from 0.0958 to about 0. Total error makes a U with minimum 0.0219 at degree 2. One claim: flexibility trades bias for variance.

Nugget 02 · A little bias can buy a lot of variance

MLE: MSE 0.05250. Laplace: bias -0.06667, MSE 0.02778. The biased estimator nearly halves the error.

What happened
The lesson splits MSE as bias² + variance: 0.00444 + 0.02333 = 0.02778, and shows the U-bottom at degree 2 with error 0.0219.
Why it matters
Unbiasedness is a virtue, not a requirement. Regularization is deliberate bias, purchased for variance.
Transferable principle
companion Judge estimators by total error, not by purity. Bias you choose beats variance you suffer.

Section 5 · C05

Penalties: shrink the weights, pay in raw fit

From the lessonC05

The computed example, 2026-10-06. With lambda = 0: w* = 0.928571, RSS = 1.928571, penalty 0, penalized objective 3.653061. With n·lambda = 2: w* = 0.812500, RSS = 2.117188, penalty 1.320313, penalized objective 3.437500. One claim: the penalty shrinks w and raises the raw RSS. The raw fit gets worse. The penalized objective gets better.

From the lessonC05 Penalty bench: the lesson's ridge toy with Sxx = 14, Sxy = 13. Move lambda and watch w shrink.

w = Sxy / (Sxx + n·lambda), n = 4. Lesson endpoints: lambda 0 gives 0.928571, n·lambda 2 gives 0.8125.

Section 6 · C06

Priors: the penalty wears a probability costume

From the lessonC06

tau² = 0.5, sigma² = 1. sigma²/tau² = 2. w_MAP = 13/16 = 0.8125. lambda_equiv = 0.5. The numbers match C05 to all digits: 0.8125 both ways. A Gaussian prior on w with variance tau² is a ridge penalty with strength sigma²/tau². Two languages, one shrinkage.

Nugget 03 · A prior is a penalty with a story

w_MAP = 0.8125 from Bayes. w* = 0.8125 from ridge. Identical to all digits, because they are the same math.

What happened
The lesson computes the MAP estimate with tau² = 0.5 and lands exactly on the penalized estimate from C05.
Why it matters
Regularization strength is a belief about weight size. The prior makes the belief explicit and tunable.
Transferable principle
companion When a penalty feels arbitrary, translate it to a prior. Beliefs are easier to argue about than lambdas.

Section 8 · C08

Cross-validation: spend data to choose lambda

From the lessonC08

The computed example, 2026-10-06. The penalty is scaled with the fold size so lambda means the same thing on 3 points as on 4. Held-out errors. At x = 0: 0.0000 both. At x = 1: 0.0059 vs 0.0297. At x = 2: 2.5600 vs 3.1777. At x = 3: 4.8400 vs 1.5148. CV mean: 1.8515 for lambda = 0, 1.1806 for n·lambda = 2. The penalty wins on unseen folds.

Section 9 · C09

Capacity: past the truth, the test error explodes

From the lessonC09

The computed example, 2026-10-06. Degree 1, 2 params: train MSE 0.03075, test MSE 2.87570. Degree 2, 3 params: train 0.01513, test 0.53133. Degree 3, 4 params: train 0.00000, test 28.56125. Train falls monotonically. Test makes the U with minimum at degree 2. One claim: capacity past the truth hurts. Degree 3 memorizes the train set exactly and pays 28.56 on test.

Nugget 04 · Zero training error can be the worst news

Degree 3: train MSE 0.00000, test MSE 28.56125. The model learned the noise perfectly and the signal not at all.

What happened
The lesson fits degrees 1 to 3: train error falls to exactly zero while test error explodes past the truth at degree 2.
Why it matters
Interpolating the training set is easy. The test set is the only exam that counts.
Transferable principle
companion Celebrate test error, never train error. A zero on train is a warning, not a win.

Section 11 · C11

Leakage: the perfect score measured the leak

From the lessonC11

The computed example, 2026-10-06. Train accuracy with the leak present: 1.0. Deploy accuracy with the leak absent: 0.6. One claim: the perfect score measured the leak. The model learned the pipeline, not the problem.

Nugget 05 · A perfect score is a suspect, not a success

Train 1.0, deploy 0.6. The 0.4 gap is the leak's signature: the model saw the answer during training.

What happened
The lesson shows a feature that exists in training but not in deployment, inflating accuracy to a perfect 1.0.
Why it matters
Leakage is the commonest cause of models that die on arrival. The train number lies. The deploy number tells.
Transferable principle
companion When accuracy looks too good, audit the features for future information. Perfection is evidence, not achievement.

Section 12 · C12

Distribution shift: the same model, a new world

From the lessonC12

The computed example, 2026-10-06. Train x range [0, 3]: MSE 0.0986. Shift test x range [4, 6]: MSE 0.5548. One claim: the same model, a new x range, more than five times the error. The model never saw the new world during training, and it shows.

Nugget 06 · Models interpolate. The world extrapolates

MSE 0.0986 on the training range, 0.5548 just outside it. The model is a local citizen with no passport.

What happened
The lesson moves the test range from [0, 3] to [4, 6] and watches error multiply by 5.6 with the same weights.
Why it matters
Deployment drift is the norm, not the exception. A model is only as good as the overlap between train and world.
Transferable principle
companion Monitor the input range in production. When the world moves, the error follows.

Nugget 07 · The whole unit in one sentence

Measure on data the model never saw, penalize complexity, and distrust perfect scores. Everything else is commentary.

What happened
Twelve sections converge: risk needs a held-out estimate, penalties buy variance with bias, leakage and shift punish the careless.
Why it matters
Every modeling choice in the later units is a generalization choice. This unit is the conscience of the course.
Transferable principle
companion Before any model ships, answer three questions: what is the held-out error, what did the penalty cost, and what could leak.
