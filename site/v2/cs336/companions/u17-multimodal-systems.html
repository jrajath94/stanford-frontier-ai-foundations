Unit 1 · Remediation

The interface view

From the lessonRemediation

A training system is three interfaces: the model (compute), the data (bytes), the rollout fleet (sequences). The slowest interface sets the pace. The lesson's toy from compute_u17.py: stage times sum to 143 ms with backward at 38.5 percent. Doubling backward speed cuts the step to 116 ms, 19.2 percent faster. Amdahl's law, not magic. Which stage do you optimize first? The biggest share. What is the max possible saving? The share itself: nothing else comes close.

Unit 2 · C01, C02

Every modality becomes tokens

From the lessonC01 §2, §5, §6

How does an image enter a language model? As tokens: split into 16x16 patches, embed each patch, lay the 256 tokens before the text. Every modality becomes a token stream. The transformer never sees pixels, only embeddings in sequence. The modality's cost is its token count. Image 224x224 / 16 = 14x14 = 196 patches (the toy uses 16x16 patches for round numbers: 256). Each patch is linearly projected to d_model. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: 256 image tokens plus 512 text = 768. The image takes 33.3 percent of the context. Images are token-hungry: 8 images take 80.0 percent of the toy context, leaving little for text. The failure case: tiny text in the image. 16x16 patches blur it beyond reading. The assumption "patches suffice" breaks. Raise resolution or patch finer.

From the lessonC02 §2, §5

A document has text, two images, more text. What is the token order? The interleaved stream: text tokens, image-1 patches, text, image-2 patches, text. Position ids run across the whole stream, attention is causal over all of it. One sequence, many modalities. The model learns cross-modal links the same way it learns long-range text links: through attention over positions. Special tokens mark modality boundaries. The loss mask (U15-C04) can target text only. The lesson's toy: 200 text + 256 image + 300 text = 756 tokens, one stream. Long multimodal streams hit the context limit fast (C01: 8 images = 80 percent). The failure case: modalities need different position handling (2D for images). Flat positions confuse spatial relations. The assumption "1D positions suffice" breaks. Add 2D-aware encodings.

Unit 3 · C03

Three pipes in series

From the lessonC03 §2, §5, §6

The GPUs idle while data loads. Whose bug? The interface contract: the data loader owes tokens/sec, the model owes tokens/sec/GPU, the cluster owes the interconnect. Three pipes in series: storage to loader to GPUs to interconnect. Throughput is the minimum. Size each pipe to the target, then verify. Required bandwidth = token rate x bytes/token. Required interconnect = the collective volume per step (U08/U09) divided by the step time. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: 2.5 Mtok/s needs 0.010 GB/s at 4.2 bytes/token. The data interface is rarely the bottleneck at this rate. Overprovisioning one pipe wastes money. The minimum decides. The failure case: the loader hits a slow storage tier at 3am. The contract held on average, not at p99. The assumption "average rates suffice" breaks. Contract the tail too.

Unit 4 · C04

Two fleets, one contract

From the lessonC04 §2, §5, §6

Why not generate and train on the same GPUs? Because the two workloads want different things: generation wants low-latency inference (U11), training wants throughput. Separation lets each use its best setup. The sync point is the weight broadcast. Two fleets, one contract: rollout workers generate with weight version v, trainers optimize to v+1, then broadcast. The version tag (U16-C10) keeps the staleness honest. The tradeoff: frequent broadcasts cost bandwidth, rare ones cost staleness. The lesson's toy: broadcast every 10 updates. Staleness KL stays under 0.01 nats on the U16-C10 toy path. The broadcast moves the full weights across the cluster. Idle time appears when the fleets desync (C05). The failure case: the broadcast fails silently and workers generate on stale weights. The KL budget blows past with no alarm. The assumption "broadcasts succeed" breaks. Checksum the weights.

Unit5

Unit 5 · C05

Two rates, one queue

From the lessonC05 §2, §5, §6

Rollouts produce 120 seq/min, the trainer consumes 100. What happens? Two rates, one queue. Utilization = min(r,c)/max(r,c). Backlog = (r-c) x time. Size the queue or balance the rates. The math gives no third option. The queue grows linearly in the rate difference. Steady state needs r below or at c, or a drain policy. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: utilization 83.3 percent, backlog grows 1200 sequences/hour. Balanced at 100/100. The queue holds sequences that age: staleness (U16-C10) grows with queue time. The failure case: the trainer stalls for an hour. The queue overflows and fresh rollouts are dropped. The assumption "the trainer never stalls" breaks. Bound the queue and shed load oldest-first.

Unit 6 · C06, C07

Pin the template, watch the fleet

From the lessonC06 §2, §5, §6

SFT used template v2, RL rollouts use v3. What breaks? The policy sees a new format: KL spikes, rewards wobble, and nobody knows if the model or the template changed. One template version per training stage, pinned in the manifest (U13-C12). Normalization (whitespace, unicode) is part of the template. Change it deliberately or not at all. The rollout config hashes the template. The trainer verifies the hash before each run. The lesson's toy: template v2 hash ab12, v3 hash cd34. The mismatch check fires before any GPU burns. Pinning slows iteration: every template tweak is a versioned change. The failure case: normalization differs between the data pipeline and the serving stack. Training sees clean text, users send raw text. The assumption "one normalization" breaks. Test with raw inputs.

From the lessonC07 §2, §5

The rollout fleet generates a million responses a day. Who watches? Controls: rollout filters (block bad generations from training), reward-model monitoring (catch drift), human review sampling, and the kill switch. Defense in depth: no single control is trusted. The filter catches the known-bad, sampling catches the unknown, the kill switch bounds the blast radius. Each control has an owner, a threshold, and a runbook. The lesson's toy: 1 percent human review of 1M rollouts = 10k reviews/day. The kill criterion: safety slice drops 2 points. Review labor and slower iteration are the price. The alternative is unmonitored optimization (U16-C11). The failure case: the filter and the policy co-adapt. The policy learns the filter's blind spots. The assumption "controls are fixed" breaks. Refresh them adversarially.

Unit 7 · C08, C09

Lineage and save points

From the lessonC08 §2, §5, §6

This checkpoint misbehaves. Which data made it? Every artifact records its parents' hashes. The chain is a Merkle-like trail from raw data to final weights. Debugging starts at the end and walks back. id_n = sha256(id_{n-1} + stage_n). Tampering anywhere changes every later id. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: the chain raw-v3 to dedup-v3 to mix-v7 to train-run-12 to sft-run-4 to rl-run-2 ends at artifact id ea98188a5dad105d. Walk the chain backward to the data. The bookkeeping is small. The discipline (every stage records) is the hard part. The failure case: a stage ran twice with different configs but one id. The chain lies. The assumption "one id per run" breaks. Make ids content-addressed.

From the lessonC09 §2, §5, §6

The cluster dies at 80 percent of training. How much is lost? The checkpoint interval decides: at most one interval's work. The full state (U15-C12) makes the resume exact. Checkpoints are save points. The interval trades write cost against redo cost. The resume replays from the last save point with identical RNG and data position. Expected waste = interval/2 steps on random failure. Write cost = state bytes / bandwidth per checkpoint. The lesson's toy: interval 1000 steps, state 84 GB (U15-C12), write bandwidth 10 GB/s. 8.4 s per checkpoint, expected waste 500 steps. Frequent checkpoints cost write time and storage. Rare ones cost redo time. The failure case: the checkpoint itself is corrupt. The resume loads garbage. The assumption "checkpoints are valid" breaks. Verify on write (read-back hash).

Unit 8 · C10, C11

Invariants and Amdahl

From the lessonC10 §2, §5, §6

Every stage passed its unit tests, but the final model is wrong. What test catches system bugs? End-to-end invariants: data lineage intact (C08), template hashes match (C06), loss reproducible from the manifest (U13-C12), evals move in the right direction. Unit tests check parts, invariants check the composition. The pipeline is correct when the invariants hold from raw data to final eval. The lesson's toy: 6 invariants, 5 pass, template hash fails. The run is rejected before eval. The suite runs per stage transition. Skipping it moves debugging to production. The failure case: the invariants are stale. They check last quarter's pipeline. The assumption "invariants are current" breaks. Version them with the pipeline.

From the lessonC11 §2, §5, §6

Where does optimization pay? The lesson's run from compute_u17.py: 143 ms step, backward 55 ms (38.5 percent). Doubling backward speed saves 27.5 ms: 19.2 percent off the step. Nothing else comes close. Profile first, optimize second. Amdahl's law: the gain is bounded by the stage's share. Optimizing a 3 percent stage is theater. New total = total - share + share/speedup. Max gain = the share itself. Profiling costs engineering time. Optimizing the wrong stage costs more. The failure case: the bottleneck moves. After fixing backward, all-reduce becomes the limit. The assumption "one bottleneck" breaks. Re-profile after each fix.

Unit 9 · C12

A toy result is a hypothesis

From the lessonC12 §2, §5, §6

The effect is +0.10 at 1x. Does it hold at 64x? The lesson's synthetic run, executed 2026-10-06: effects +0.10, +0.06, +0.02, -0.03 at 1x, 4x, 16x, 64x. The sign flips: the small-scale conclusion does not transfer. Scale changes the regime: different bottlenecks, different noise, different optima. A toy result is a hypothesis about large scale, never a conclusion. Test at 2-3 scales, watch the trend, never extrapolate the sign. Multi-scale tests cost runs. Single-scale claims cost credibility. The failure case: the trend looks stable across 1x-16x then flips at 64x. The tested range was too narrow. The assumption "the trend continues" breaks. Test the widest affordable range.
