Section 1 · Remediation

Loss, gradient, update

From the lessonRemediation

Training minimizes the mean negative log probability of the true next token (cross-entropy). One step: compute the loss on a batch, differentiate (reverse mode), and move parameters against the gradient scaled by the learning rate. Adam keeps running averages of the gradient (m) and its square (v) and scales each parameter's update by 1/sqrt(v).

From the lessonRemediation

The worked check the lesson opens with: logits [1000, 1001, 999], true class 1. Naive softmax overflows to nan. The stable form (subtract max first) gives CE = 0.4076. From visuals/compute_u05.py, executed 2026-10-06.

Section 2 · C01

Stable cross-entropy: subtract the max

From the lessonC01 §2, §3, §5

Why does the loss go to nan on the first step? Cross-entropy = log(sum(exp(logits))) - logit_true. The exp of large numbers overflows. Subtracting the max first keeps every exp in [0, 1] without changing the math: log(sum(exp(x))) = m + log(sum(exp(x - m))) with m = max(x). Then CE = lse - x_true. The gradient is softmax(logits) - one_hot: the familiar form falls out.

From the lessonC01 §5 Live stable-CE toy. Type logits (comma separated) and the true class. The toy runs the lesson's stable form.

Press the button.

CE = (max + log(sum(exp(x - max)))) - x_true. The naive path is not run here: exp(1001) overflows by design.

Nugget 1 · From the lessonC01 §11

Computing softmax then log separately lets exp overflow before log can rescue it. The loss is nan from step one.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "the framework handles it" breaks for hand-rolled losses. Always fuse.
Transfer companion
Fuse the numerically fragile chain into one stable kernel. Never split exp from its log.

From the lessonC01 §9, §10

Costs: O(B*T*V) time and memory for the full logits. The stability is free. At extreme V, sampled softmax or hierarchical variants approximate. Exact stable CE is the default.

Section 3 · C02

The label shift: predict the next token

From the lessonC02 §2, §3, §5, §6

What is the target at position t? The toy from compute_u05.py: ids [10, 20, 30, 40] give inputs [10, 20, 30] and labels [20, 30, 40]. Predict the next token. The model reads tokens 0..T-2 and is scored on tokens 1..T-1. Training feeds the true past (teacher forcing), and the causal mask (U03-C04) keeps the prediction honest. The autoregressive factorization P(x) = product over t of P(x_t | x before t) becomes a classification task per position. The shift aligns logits[t] with ids[t+1].

Nugget 2 · From the lessonC02 §11

Shift the wrong way (labels = inputs) and the model learns the identity function. Loss drops to near zero, and sampling produces repetitions of the prompt.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "the shift is obviously right" breaks silently. The symptom is a too-good loss.
Transfer companion
Distrust a loss that looks too good. Check what the model is actually asked to predict.

From the lessonC02 §9, §10

Free to implement. Getting it wrong off-by-one trains the model to predict the current token: loss looks fine, generation babbles. Prefix-LM and fill-in-the-middle use different shifts. The standard shift is for autoregressive LMs.

Section 4 · C03, C05

AdamW: adaptive steps, then the correction

From the lessonC03 §2, §3, §5

Why not plain SGD? Adam tracks a moving average of gradients (m, direction with momentum) and of squared gradients (v, per-parameter scale). Each parameter steps by m/sqrt(v): fast for rare features, calm for noisy ones. m_t = b1*m_{t-1} + (1-b1)*g_t. v_t = b2*v_{t-1} + (1-b2)*g_t^2. Bias correction divides by (1-b^t) (C05). AdamW update: w -= lr * (mhat/(sqrt(vhat)+eps) + lam*w).

From the lessonC03 §6, §9

The lesson's hand step, executed 2026-10-06: one AdamW step moves w=[1.0, -0.5] to [0.99899, -0.498995] with per-parameter adaptive scaling. Correction factors at t=1: 10.0 and 1000.0. Costs: two extra states per parameter, the 16 bytes of U02-C05. Compute is elementwise: cheap. AdamW is the default for transformers.

From the lessonC05 §2, §3, §5, §6

Why divide by (1-b^t)? m and v start at zero, so early averages are biased toward zero. Dividing unbiases them: at t=1 the averages equal the first gradient exactly. E[m_t] = (1-b^t) E[g]: the geometric series sums to (1-b^t)/(1-b), times (1-b). The lesson's numbers: 1/(1-0.9) = 10.0, 1/(1-0.999) = 1000.0 at t=1. Without them the first updates would be 10x and 1000x too small.

Nugget 3 · From the lessonC03 §11, C05 §11

eps = 0 with a zero v (dead parameter) means division by zero: nan. And skipping bias correction with a large lr makes the first steps tiny, then the effective step jumps as (1-b^t) approaches 1, mimicking a warmup spike that destabilizes.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "v stays positive" breaks at init without eps: eps is load-bearing. The assumption "correction hardly matters" breaks at large lr.
Transfer companion
Keep the exact correction over the convenient approximation. Two scalar ops buy the first hundred steps.

From the lessonC03 §10, C05 §10

Alternatives to AdamW: SGD with momentum (less memory, needs more tuning), Lion, Adafactor (C09). Warmup (C07) partially covers the same need as bias correction, and some implementations skip correction and rely on warmup. Keep the correction: it is exact.

Section 5 · C04

Weight decay is not L2 under Adam

From the lessonC04 §2, §3, §5

Is weight_decay just L2? L2 adds lam*w to the gradient, so Adam's adaptive denominator scales the regularization too: parameters with large gradients get less decay. Decoupled decay (AdamW) subtracts lam*w directly: every parameter decays at the same rate. Adam+L2: w -= lr * (m(g+lam*w))/sqrt(v). AdamW: w -= lr * m(g)/sqrt(v) - lr*lam*w. The lam*w term escapes the adaptive scaling in AdamW.

From the lessonC04 §6

The lesson's toy, executed 2026-10-06: with w=0.5, g=0.1, lam=0.1, AdamW applies decay 0.0500 outside the adaptive scaling, while L2-style adds 0.05 to the gradient, giving an L2-style grad of 0.1500 entangled with 1/sqrt(v).

Nugget 4 · From the lessonC04 §11

Decaying the norms and biases (or the embedding) regularizes parameters that should not be regularized that way. The standard recipe excludes them.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "decay everything" breaks. Apply decay to matrices only.
Transfer companion
Regularization is not uniform by default. Scope it to the parameters it was designed for.

From the lessonC04 §9, §10

Identical compute. The difference is purely in dynamics. With SGD, L2 and decoupled decay coincide. Use AdamW's decoupled form with Adam.

Section 6 · C06

Clipping: cap the step, keep the direction

From the lessonC06 §2, §3, §5, §6

What stops one bad batch from wrecking the weights? Clipping rescales the whole gradient when its global norm exceeds a threshold: g *= min(1, c/norm). It caps the step size without changing the direction. The update norm is bounded by lr*c regardless of the raw gradient. This prevents explosion from rare spikes: bad batches, numerical events. The lesson's toy, executed 2026-10-06: [3, 4, 0, ...] has norm 5.0, clipped to norm 1.0000. Direction kept, magnitude capped.

Nugget 5 · From the lessonC06 §11

Clipping every step, with c far below the typical norm, trains on directions only at an effectively fixed step size. Worse, it hides a real instability, like bad data.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "clipping is a safety net" breaks when it becomes the regime. Log the clip frequency.
Transfer companion
A safety mechanism that fires constantly is a symptom, not a setting. Measure how often it fires.

From the lessonC06 §9, §10

One extra norm over parameters per step: cheap. Alternatives: per-parameter clipping, adaptive clipping. Global-norm is the standard.

Section 7 · C07

Schedules: warmup, cosine, WSD

From the lessonC07 §2, §3, §5

Why not a constant lr? Warmup starts at 0 and ramps up: early gradients are noisy and moments uninitialized. Cosine decays smoothly to a floor: large steps early, fine steps late. WSD (warmup, stable, decay) holds constant after warmup, then decays fast over the last 10 to 20 percent: it lets you stop early or extend without retuning the decay horizon. Warmup: lr = peak * t/warm. Cosine: floor + 0.5*(peak-floor)*(1+cos(pi*(t-warm)/(total-warm))).

From the lessonC07 §6

The lesson's toy (warmup 100, total 1000, peak 3e-4, floor 3e-5), executed 2026-10-06: lr at steps 0/50/100/550/1000 = 0, 1.5e-4, 3e-4, 1.65e-4, 3e-5.

Nugget 6 · From the lessonC07 §9, §11

Cosine with the wrong total, when the job stops at half, never decays. The final model is under-converged.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "total is known" breaks on preempted runs. WSD exists for this. The schedule is the cheapest hyperparameter with the largest effect.
Transfer companion
When the horizon is uncertain, pick the schedule that survives an early stop.

From the lessonC07 §10

Selection boundary: cosine for fixed budgets, WSD for open-ended runs. Older: constant lr with decay-on-plateau, linear decay.

Section 8 · C08

Accumulation: the big batch, in pieces

From the lessonC08 §2, §3, §5, §6

The batch does not fit. Now what? Gradient accumulation sums (or averages) microbatch gradients before the optimizer step. The math equals one big batch, the memory equals one microbatch. The price is time: a accumulation steps per update. grad(mean over a*m samples) = mean over a of grad(microbatch means). Summing microbatch grads and dividing by a is algebraically identical. The lesson's toy, executed 2026-10-06: ([0.2, 0.4] + [0.6, -0.2]) / 2 = [0.4, 0.1] exactly.

Nugget 7 · From the lessonC08 §11

Forgetting to divide by a multiplies the effective lr by a, and training diverges.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "sum is enough" breaks. The mean is the contract. Some frameworks sum the loss, so check the convention.
Transfer companion
When the code splits a sum into pieces, the reassembly rule is part of the correctness proof.

From the lessonC08 §9, §10

Memory of one microbatch, time of a. Batchnorm-style statistics (not used in LMs) would differ. Norms per microbatch are fine. Alternatives: a smaller global batch (changes optimization dynamics), a distributed batch (U08).

Section 9 · C09, C10

Other optimizers, and muP

From the lessonC09 §2, §3, §5

Is AdamW the only answer? All optimizers are update rules with states. SGD with momentum: velocity = mu*v + g, step lr*v, with 4 bytes/param of state. Lion: sign(momentum) steps, smaller state. Adafactor: factored second moments for memory. Each is a branch: verify against its paper before trusting a reimplementation. The lab hand-steps SGD+m and Lion on w=[1.0], g=[0.5] with default hparams. Values are in the lab key. The trap: copying Lion's lr from an AdamW recipe diverges the run. Lion's sign updates need much smaller lr. The assumption "lr transfers across optimizers" breaks. Retune.

From the lessonC10 §2, §3, §5

Why retune the lr for every model size? muP is a parameterization: each layer's init and lr scale with width so that feature learning stays O(1) per coordinate. Tune on the small model, the large model inherits the hyperparameters. Standard param: init 1/fan_in, lr O(1). muP: hidden layers init O(1/fan_in) with lr O(1/n), output layer init O(1/n) with lr O(1). The scalings keep updates width-independent in the infinite-width limit. The lab tabulates init std and lr multiplier for widths 128/512/2048 under standard versus muP rules.

Nugget 8 · From the lessonC10 §11

Mixing muP init with standard lr makes the scales fight: training diverges or stalls.

Why it matters companion
The assumption "one of the two suffices" breaks. Both must match.
Transfer companion
Coupled scalings travel as a pair. Adopt half the pair and you adopt none of it.

From the lessonC09 §9, §10, C10 §9, §10

AdamW stays the default. Alternatives are for memory-constrained or research-comparison settings, verified first. muP pays off when tuning is expensive. At moderate scale, standard parameterization with fresh tuning per size remains the common practice.

Section 10 · C11, C12, synthesis

Checkpoints, RNG, and the gradient check

From the lessonC11 §2, §3, §5, §6

What must a checkpoint contain to resume bit-identically? The training state: weights, optimizer moments, step, scheduler state, RNG states, data loader position. Missing any one breaks exact resumption, and the RNG is the most commonly forgotten. Dropout masks, data shuffling, and init noise all draw from the RNG. Restoring the exact state continues the identical stream. The lesson's toy, executed 2026-10-06: save state, draw 3 numbers, restore, draw again, identical.

From the lessonC11 §11

Resuming with a fresh RNG changes the data order and dropout. The loss jumps, and the run looks like a hyperparameter change. The assumption "weights are enough" breaks. The symptom mimics a bad resume. Weights-only checkpoints are fine for eval, not for resume.

From the lessonC12 §2, §3, §5, §6

How do you know the backward pass is right? Finite differences are the ground truth for gradients: (f(w+e) - f(w-e)) / 2e, with O(e^2) error for central differences. Compare relatively: |a-n| / (|a|+|n|) < 1e-5. The lab run on a (4, 3) linear layer: max relative error 1.88e-10 in fp64. Loss invariants are cheap alarms: loss finite, loss decreases on a tiny overfit batch, gradient norm finite and nonzero. Checking in fp16 lets rounding dominate and fails correct code. Use fp64.
