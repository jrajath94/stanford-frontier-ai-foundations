Section 1 · Remediation

Uncertainty first

From the lessonRemediation

A score without an interval is a rumor. For accuracy p on n items, SE = sqrt(p(1-p)/n) and the 95 percent interval is p plus or minus 1.96 SE. The lesson's toy: n=500, p=0.72 gives SE 0.020 and [0.681, 0.759]. Five synthetic seeds range 0.034, inside the band. This formula is the load-bearing wall of the unit: slices (C10), seeds (C09), and formats (C06) all get judged against intervals like this one.

Section 2 · C01, C02

Perplexity, and whether the test leaked

From the lessonC01 §2, §5, §6

Loss 2.3: what does that mean? Perplexity = exp(mean negative log likelihood). It is the geometric mean of 1/p over tokens. The effective branching factor: the model is as confused as if it chose uniformly among 9.97 tokens. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: loss 2.3 gives 9.97, 1.7 gives 5.47, 1.0 gives 2.72. Lower is better. It measures fit to the held-out distribution, nothing more. Perplexity averages over all tokens equally, but tasks care about some tokens more. It also depends on the tokenizer (C03). Bits per byte (C03) is for cross-tokenizer comparison. Task accuracy (C05) is for capability claims. The failure case: the held-out set leaks into training (C02). Perplexity looks great and means nothing. The assumption "held-out" breaks. Check provenance.

From the lessonC02 §2, §5, §6

Did the benchmark leak into training? Contamination is memorization wearing an eval costume. Exact-match overlap finds verbatim leaks. A canary (a unique string inserted at crawl time) proves the pipeline carries eval text into training. Hash every benchmark n-gram, probe the training corpus index. The canary is found by literal search. Both are detection, not prevention. The lesson's toy on a synthetic corpus of 10,000 docs with 300 planted benchmark items, executed 2026-10-06: 100.0 percent overlap by exact match, a planted unique 8-gram canary recovered with recall 1.00. Paraphrased leaks evade exact match. Fuzzy matching costs compute and brings false positives. The failure case: the benchmark is reworded in training. Exact overlap reads 0 percent while the model still memorized the content. The assumption "leaks are verbatim" breaks. Add near-dup detection.

Section 3 · C03

The common denominator

From the lessonC03 §2, §5, §6

Tokenizer A gives loss 2.00, B gives 2.20. Is A better? Per-token loss divides by the tokenizer's own token count. Bits per byte divides by bytes, which no tokenizer controls. It is the common denominator. Total nats = loss x tokens. Divide by bytes, convert nats to bits. Tokenizer differences cancel out of the denominator. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: bits per byte are A 0.687, B 0.605. B wins: it uses fewer tokens per byte. Bits per byte still depends on the byte content (language, domain). Compare on the same corpus. The failure case: different corpora. B looks better because its corpus is easier. The assumption "same bytes" breaks. Fix the corpus first.

Nugget 4 · From the lessonC03

Bits per byte is the common denominator no tokenizer controls. B at 0.605 beats A at 0.687.

Why it matters companion
Per-token rankings can reverse under a fair denominator. Cross-tokenizer claims without it are invalid.
Transfer companion
When two systems count differently, find the denominator neither controls.

Section 4 · C04, C05

Two audiences, one portfolio

From the lessonC04 §2, §5, §6

The team iterates daily on a dev set and reports monthly on a hidden set. Why two sets? Because tuning on the reported set lets the test set train the model, with extra steps. Internal evals steer: fast, frequent, on data the team can inspect. External evals judge: held-out, versioned, run by someone else. Mixing the two inflates the headline number. Every decision made against a set consumes some of its statistical power. The lesson's toy: 20 daily decisions against the dev set, each with 5 percent false-positive risk. The chance at least one bogus "improvement" survives is 1 - 0.95^20 = 64 percent. Hidden sets cost curation and guarding. Leaked external sets cost the entire evaluation program. The failure case: the external set is "hidden" but its items appear in training data (C02). The assumption "hidden means unseen" breaks. Audit provenance.

From the lessonC05 §2, §5, §6

The model scores 72 percent average. What does that mean? A suite is a portfolio of small bets. The average is the headline, the slices are the truth. Small slices have wide intervals (remediation). The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: slices read math 0.80 (n=40), code 0.65 (n=60), all 0.72 (n=500). The average hides a 15-point spread. The macro average weights tasks equally regardless of size. A 5-point gain on a 40-item slice is 2 items: noise. Half-widths: 0.124, 0.121, 0.039. Suites cost curation and become stale as models saturate them. A saturated suite measures nothing. The failure case: the suite is contaminated (C02). The average is memorization, not capability. The assumption "the suite is clean" breaks. Audit first.

Section 5 · C06, C07

The prompt is part of the instrument

From the lessonC06 §2, §5, §6

Same questions, three prompt formats. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: accuracies 0.717, 0.723, 0.657. The format moves the score by 6.6 points, more than many model differences. The prompt is part of the instrument. Change the instrument, change the reading. Scaffolds (few-shot examples, chain-of-thought cues) are stronger instruments with bigger effects. Hold the questions and the model fixed, vary only the format. Format search is prompt tuning on the eval set: it consumes statistical power (C04). One fixed format for all models is fair but may handicap some. Per-model best format shows potential but is not comparable. The failure case: the scaffold contains the answer pattern. The eval measures pattern matching, not reasoning. The assumption "the scaffold is neutral" breaks. Ablate the scaffold.

From the lessonC07 §2, §5

The score was measured at temperature 0.7, top-p 0.9. Is it comparable to a greedy score? No. The settings are part of the measurement, like the exposure time on a photograph. Greedy decoding measures the mode. Sampling measures the distribution. Pass@k needs sampling. Exact-match accuracy usually wants greedy. The setting must match the metric's intent. The score is a function f(model, items, settings): report all three arguments. From U11-C08: temp 0.5 gives top-1 0.865, temp 2.0 gives 0.455. The same model looks 40 points more confident under the colder setting. The failure case: max tokens too small. Correct answers get cut off and score 0. The assumption "the setting does not censor" breaks. Check length histograms.

Nugget 5 · From the lessonC06

Why it matters companion

Section 6 · C08, C09

Judges and seeds

From the lessonC08 §2, §5, §6

The judge agrees with humans 83 percent of the time. Good enough? A judge is a measurement device with its own error rate. Agreement without a chance correction flatters easy tasks. Kappa asks: how much better than coin-flipping the base rate? kappa = (agree - expected) / (1 - expected), where expected is the chance agreement from the marginal label rates. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: agreement 0.83, Cohen kappa 0.63. Kappa corrects for chance: 0.63 is substantial but not near-perfect. Human labels for the calibration set are the real cost. An uncalibrated judge is a rumor with a GPU bill. The failure case: the judge prefers longer answers and the models learn to ramble. The metric is gamed (U16-C11). The assumption "the judge is neutral" breaks. Audit for length bias.

From the lessonC09 §2, §5, §6

The score moved 3 points between runs. Did the model change? Sampling and data order inject noise. The seed band is the ruler: differences smaller than the band are not differences. The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: five synthetic seeds give accuracies [0.724, 0.708, 0.730, 0.696, 0.724], range 0.034. A 3-point move is inside seed noise. Each run is a binomial draw with n=500, p=0.72. The spread of the draws follows the SE 0.020. The observed range 0.034 is consistent. Five full eval runs cost 5x. For big suites, 3 seeds is the common compromise. The failure case: seeds correlate through shared data order. The band understates the true variation. The assumption "seeds are independent" breaks. Vary the data split too.

Nugget 6 · From the lessonC08

Raw agreement flatters easy tasks. The chance-corrected number is the honest one.
Transfer companion

Section 7 · C10

Refuse to rank on small slices

From the lessonC10 §2, §5, §6

Math 0.80 beats code 0.65. Real? The lesson's run, executed 2026-10-06: half-widths are 0.124 and 0.121. The 0.15 gap is inside the combined noise: not a real lead on the toy. Slices are small n, small n means wide bands. The slice table with intervals is the honest report. The slice table without them is a trap. The gap's SE is the root-sum-square of the two slice SEs. Full numbers: math 0.80 plus or minus 0.124, code 0.65 plus or minus 0.121, all 0.72 plus or minus 0.039. Slices multiply the multiple-comparison problem: with 20 slices, one will "win" by chance. The failure case: slices defined after seeing the results (slicing to the headline). The assumption "slices are predeclared" breaks. Preregister slices (U10-C10).

Section 8 · C11

The laboratory and the field

From the lessonC11 §2, §5

The model aces the benchmark and flops in production. What broke? Usually one of: the prompt distribution differs, the task format differs, or the grading differs from user satisfaction. A benchmark is a laboratory. Deployment is the field. Validity is the bridge: same inputs, same format, same success criterion. Every difference is a tax on the score's meaning. The lesson's toy: benchmark prompts average 40 tokens, production prompts average 400. The length gap alone can move scores by double digits (C06 showed 6.6 points from format alone). Production sampling costs privacy review and annotation. Skipping it costs surprise failures. The failure case: the eval is in English, production is multilingual. The benchmark says 0.90, the field says 0.60. The assumption "language matches" breaks. Slice by language (C10).

Section 9 · C12

When the worse model wins

From the lessonC12 §2, §5, §6

Model B scores higher but costs more per token. Which ships? Accuracy is the numerator story. Cost per correct task divides by the price of being right. Verbose correct answers can lose to terse mostly-correct ones. Cost per task = (tokens/1000 x price) / accuracy. Expected cost of one success under the toy pricing. The lesson's run at a toy price of $0.002 per 1k tokens, executed 2026-10-06: model A (800 tokens, 0.72 acc) costs $0.0022 per correct task. Model B (2400 tokens, 0.85 acc) costs $0.0056. A wins on economics despite losing on accuracy. The metric ignores latency, quality of wrong answers, and user trust. It is one column of the decision, not the decision. The failure case: wrong answers have real costs (support tickets, harm). The cheapest correct task can be the most expensive mistake. The assumption "only correct tasks matter" breaks. Add the error cost.

Nugget 8 · From the lessonC12
