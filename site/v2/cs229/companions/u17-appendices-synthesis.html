Unit 1 · SL-01 · C01

Four identities the derivations leaned on

From the lessonSL-01 §Source anchor

A.1.1: independent standard Gaussian vectors ε₁..ε_t in R^d with real weights a_s: Σ_s a_s ε_s ∼ N(0, (Σ_s a_s²) I). Sums of independent Gaussians stay Gaussian. Variances add with squared weights. Used for noise accumulation (U12 diffusion, U16 LQG). A.1.2: conditioning a joint Gaussian. If (A, B) is jointly Gaussian, then A | B = b ∼ N(μ_A + Σ_AB Σ_BB⁻¹ (b − μ_B), Σ_AA − Σ_AB Σ_BB⁻¹ Σ_BA). The Kalman update (U16) is this lemma in disguise. A.1.3: KL between Gaussians with the same covariance Σ: KL(N(m₁, Σ) || N(m₂, Σ)) = (1/2)(m₁ − m₂)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (m₁ − m₂). Only the means matter. The normalizing constants cancel. A.1.4: chain rule for KL. KL(q || p) = KL(q(x) || p(x)) + E_{q(x)}[KL(q(y|x) || p(y|x))]. For sequences it telescopes into a sum of per-step conditional KLs (A.13). This is the identity behind per-token KL penalties in RLVR (U15, equation 18.2): the sequence KL is a sum of token KLs.

P = N([1, 0], I₂), Q = N([0, 0], I₂)KL(P || Q)

Only the means matter. With the same covariance, the normalizing constants cancel.

Unit 2 · SL-02 · C02

Historical supplement: factor analysis

From the lessonSL-02 §Source anchor, §The model, §Where it survives

Boundary: not in the 2026 notes. Older CS229 handout sets included a factor analysis section. The 2026 notes cite "Factor Analysis notes" once (chapter 20, Kalman derivation) without including them. Taught here as a labeled historical supplement from standard background. The model: x = μ + Λz + ε, z ∼ N(0, I_k), ε ∼ N(0, Ψ) with Ψ diagonal. Marginal: x ∼ N(μ, ΛΛᵀ + Ψ). FA explains covariance as low-rank signal plus independent noise per coordinate. PCA (U11) is the deterministic cousin: FA keeps a noise model, which is why the EM algorithm for FA resembles the EM of U10. Where it survives: the Kalman filter's Gaussian conditioning (U16 SL-06) is the same mathematics. The 2026 notes kept the machinery and dropped the standalone chapter.

Unit 3 · SL-03 · C03

Historical supplement: online learning and the perceptron

From the lessonSL-03 §Source anchor, §The online protocol, §Why it mattered historically

Boundary: not in the 2026 notes as a standalone section. The perceptron appears in U03 (C06) as a classifier. The online protocol below is standard background, labeled as a historical supplement. Data arrives one example at a time. The learner predicts, then sees the label, then updates. No second pass. The perceptron update: on a mistake on (x, y) with y in {+1, −1}, w := w + yx. Mistake-bound: if the data is separable with margin γ and ||x|| ≤ R, the perceptron makes at most (R/γ)² mistakes, then stops updating. Finite mistakes on separable data, no distributional assumptions. It is the ancestor of stochastic gradient updates: one example, one gradient step, no batch. The 2026 notes teach SGD inside LMS (U02) and keep the perceptron as a classifier, but the online regret viewpoint is gone.

Unit 4 · SL-04 · C04

Historical supplement: hidden Markov models

From the lessonSL-04 §Source anchor, §The model, §Where it survives

Boundary: not in the 2026 notes. Older CS229 sets covered hidden Markov models. Taught here as a labeled historical supplement from standard background. Latent states z₁..z_T discrete, Markov transitions P(z_t | z_{t-1}), observations x_t with P(x_t | z_t). Three classical problems: (1) likelihood via the forward algorithm (dynamic programming over the state trellis), (2) most likely path via Viterbi (the max version of forward), (3) parameter learning via Baum-Welch, which is EM (U10) with the E step computed by forward-backward. Where it survives: the 2026 notes replaced discrete latent sequences with continuous ones: state-space models (U16 LQG), diffusion (U12), and autoregressive latent traces (U15 RLVR). The forward-backward idea survives as message passing, and EM survives as the general latent-variable algorithm.

Unit 5 · SL-05 · C05

Historical supplement: Gaussian processes

From the lessonSL-05 §Source anchor, §The model, §Where it survives

Boundary: not in the 2026 notes. Older CS229 sets covered Gaussian processes. Taught here as a labeled historical supplement from standard background. A GP is a distribution over functions: any finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian, f ∼ GP(m, k) with kernel k. Given observations y at X with Gaussian noise, the posterior at a new x* is Gaussian with mean k_*ᵀ(K + σ²I)⁻¹y and variance k(x*, x*) − k_*ᵀ(K + σ²I)⁻¹k_*. Prediction comes with calibrated uncertainty: the variance grows away from the data. Where it survives: kernel methods (U05) share the kernel. The 2026 notes dropped GPs as the course moved toward deep generative models and LLMs, where uncertainty quantification is weaker but scale is larger.

Unit 8 · SL-08 · C08

Original baseline implementations

From the lessonSL-08 §Source anchor, §The baseline discipline, §Why baselines come first

Boundary: the 2026 notes contain no code. These are original reference implementations written for this course, tested, not copied from any source. Three reference baselines, each under 30 lines of numpy, each with a test: (1) ridge regression (U02): θ = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹Xᵀy. Test: on data from a known linear model, the estimate recovers θ within tolerance. (2) k-means one iteration (U10): assign each point to the nearest center, recompute centers as means. Test: the objective never increases after an iteration. (3) value iteration (U15): the two-state toy. Test: error ratio 0.9 per sweep. Why baselines come first: a new method that cannot beat ridge on a linear problem, k-means on blobs, or value iteration on a tiny MDP has not earned its complexity.

Unit 9 · SL-09 · C09

Ablations: the protocol

From the lessonSL-09 §Source anchor, §The protocol, §What an ablation proves

An ablation removes or changes one component and measures the effect. Four rules: (1) one factor at a time. (2) matched budgets: same data, same compute, same tuning effort for every arm. (3) predict first: write down the expected direction before running. (4) report negative results: an ablation that shows no effect is evidence, not failure. What an ablation proves: it proves that the component matters for the measured metric under the tested conditions. It does not prove the component is necessary in general, and it does not prove the mechanism. "X helps on this task" is the claim. "X is the reason the method works" is a stronger claim needing more experiments. Worked template from the lesson: claim: quadratic features beat linear features in fitted value iteration. Arms: linear φ, quadratic φ, tabular grid baseline. Budget: same sampled states, same simulator calls. Metric: closed-loop return over 20 seeds. Prediction: quadratic wins, tabular is competitive but expensive.

Unit 10 · SL-10 · C10

Uncertainty: the reporting standard

From the lessonSL-10 §Source anchor, §The reporting standard, §What the intervals mean, §The honesty rules

Every measured number in this course carries: the sample size (seeds, examples), the spread (standard deviation or a bootstrap interval), and the comparison it supports. "Accuracy 0.83" is incomplete. "Accuracy 0.83 +/- 0.02 over 10 seeds, baseline 0.79 +/- 0.02" is a claim. A standard error bars the estimate of the mean. A bootstrap interval bars it without Gaussian assumptions. Neither bars the generalization gap to new data. The honesty rules: (1) never report the best seed as the result. Report the mean and spread, or all seeds. (2) A difference smaller than the error bars is not a win. It is noise. (3) Failed runs count. Dropping the seeds that diverged and reporting the rest is selection bias.

Unit 11 · SL-11 · C11

Oral defense: the preparation protocol

From the lessonSL-11 §Source anchor, §The preparation protocol, §What the examiners test

(1) Pick one mechanism per unit (17 mechanisms). (2) For each, write the one-sentence definition, the toy with numbers, and the derivation's key step from memory. Closed book. (3) For each, state one assumption and one counterexample that breaks it. (4) For each, state the nearest alternative and the exact boundary where you switch. (5) Defend against the debug question: given a symptom, name the cause and the check. What the examiners test: not recall. Transfer: the same mechanism under changed constraints. A defense answer has four parts: the claim, the justification, the boundary, and the check. From the lesson's failure case: preparing 17 definitions and no toys. Definitions do not survive the second follow-up. The toy does.

Unit12

Unit 12 · SL-12 · C12

The deployment gap map

From the lessonSL-12 §Source anchor, §The gaps, §The acceptance gate

The gaps: (1) train/serve skew: the serving distribution differs from the training distribution. Monitor it, do not assume it away. (2) Latency and cost: the course optimizes accuracy. Production optimizes accuracy per dollar per millisecond. (3) Feedback loops: a deployed model's outputs become its future inputs. (4) Reward validity at scale: the verifier or metric that was valid offline degrades under adversarial or shifting use. (5) Rollback: a bad deploy must be reversible in minutes. No rollback plan is not a deployment plan. (6) Ownership: someone is on call for the model's failures. A model with no owner is a liability, not an asset. The acceptance gate: before any course method ships: (1) an offline baseline it beats with matched budgets, (2) a shadow or canary evaluation on live traffic, (3) monitored metrics with alerts, (4) a rollback procedure tested at least once, (5) a named owner.
