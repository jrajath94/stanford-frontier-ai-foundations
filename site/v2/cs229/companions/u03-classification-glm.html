The sigmoid: squeeze a line into a probability

From the lessonSL-01 §Source anchor, §Mental model

Linear regression predicts 1.7 for a label that can only be 0 or 1. What function squeezes a line into a probability? The logit is the raw score: any real number. The sigmoid maps it to (0, 1): a probability. The log odds is the natural scale for the update math. The probability is the natural scale for decisions.

From the lessonSL-01 §Objects, §Derivation

The model: P(y = 1 | x, θ) = h_θ(x) = g(θᵀx), with g(t) = 1/(1 + e^(−t)). The derivative is g′(t) = g(t)(1 − g(t)). The compact Bernoulli likelihood: p(y | x, θ) = h^y (1 − h)^(1−y). The gradient of the log likelihood for one example: dl/dθⱼ = (y − h) xⱼ. The update: θⱼ := θⱼ + α (y⁽ⁱ⁾ − h_θ(x⁽ⁱ⁾)) x⁽ⁱ⁾ⱼ. It looks like LMS, but h is nonlinear in θ (notes page 24).

From the lessonSL-01 §Correctness checks, §Failure case

Three checks. h sits in (0, 1) always. Outside means a bug. The gradient (y − h)x has the shape of θ. At h = y the gradient is zero: no update on a perfect confident prediction. The failure case the notes reject on page 22: fitting linear regression to 0/1 labels. Predictions leave [0, 1], and the squared error punishes confident correct answers.

Why it matters. Shape similarity tempts a false equivalence. The convergence story, the loss surface, and the diagnostics all differ because the model differs. Read the formula, not the silhouette.

Transfer. Two systems can share an interface and differ in kind. The shared term (y − h)x is the interface. The nonlinearity is the kind.

The stable loss: never form the probability

From the lessonSL-02 §Source anchor, §Mental model

Your code computes log(sigmoid(1000)). What happens? sigmoid(1000) rounds to exactly 1.0 in float64. log(1) = 0, but the true answer is not zero. Worse, 1 − sigmoid(1000) = 0 and log(0) = −inf. The naive formula dies at the extremes. The stable form never forms the probability.

From the lessonSL-02 §Derivation

The loss: y × softplus(−t) + (1 − y) × softplus(t), with softplus(t) = log(1 + e^t) computed as max(t, 0) + log(1 + e^(−|t|)). No overflow for any finite t. The lesson's remark 2.1.1 (page 24) gives this form. At t = 0 the loss is log 2 = 0.6931 for either label.

From the lessonSL-02 §Failure case

The most common logistic regression bug: the naive formula inside a training loop. The first confident example produces −inf, the gradient becomes nan, and every parameter turns to nan within one step.

2Nugget 2 · From the lesson SL-02 §Failure case
Nugget 2 · From the lesson SL-02 §Failure case
The lesson

One −inf in one step turns every parameter to nan within one more step. The stable form is not an optimization. It is correctness.

Why it matters. Extreme inputs are not edge cases in training. Confident examples arrive on the first epoch. Code that dies on them dies immediately, not eventually.

Transfer. Rewrite any formula that subtracts two nearly equal floats or logs a computed probability. The algebra that avoids the catastrophe is the real implementation.

Softmax: three probabilities that sum to 1

From the lessonSL-03 §Source anchor, §Mental model

Three classes: spam, personal, work. How do we get three probabilities that sum to 1? Score each class: tᵢ = θᵢᵀx. Exponentiate to make them positive, divide by the sum to normalize. The result is a probability vector. Cross-entropy then asks: how much probability did the true class get? Its gradient is beautifully simple: predicted minus true.

From the lessonSL-03 §Derivation

Loss: ℓ = −t_y + log Σⱼ e^(tⱼ). Gradient: dℓ/dtᵢ = φᵢ − 1{y = i}. Chain rule to θᵢ: dℓ/dθᵢ = (φᵢ − 1{y = i}) x (notes page 27). Same (predicted − true) × x shape as the binary case. The stable softmax subtracts max(t) before exponentiating: same φ, no overflow. Naive exp on t = [1000, 1001] overflows to inf and φ = nan. The max-subtraction trick is not optional.

Unit 4 · SL-04 · C06

The perceptron: threshold, mistakes, no probabilities

From the lessonSL-04 §Source anchor, §Mental model

What if the sigmoid is replaced by a hard step: 1 if the score is non-negative, else 0? The perceptron predicts with a threshold and updates only on mistakes: θ := θ + α (y − h) x with h in {0, 1}. No probabilities, no likelihood, no confidence. It is a mistake-driven rule from the 1960s, kept because it starts the learning-theory analysis (U08).

From the lessonSL-04 §Failure case

Two limits. Non-separable data: the perceptron cycles forever, never settling. Logistic regression degrades gracefully instead. And the threshold has zero gradient almost everywhere, so no gradient method can train through it.

The perceptron learns from mistakes only. No mistake, no update. No probabilities, no confidence, no likelihood.

Unit 5 · SL-05 · C07

Newton: use the curvature, stride further

From the lessonSL-05 §Source anchor, §Mental model

Gradient ascent walks uphill with a fixed step size. Can we use the curvature to pick the step? Newton fits a quadratic bowl to the function at the current point and jumps to the bowl's top. One step is exact on a true quadratic. The price: the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives, at O(n³) per step to invert.

From the lessonSL-05 §Objects and derivation

Maximize ℓ(θ). Newton: θ := θ − H⁻¹ grad ℓ, with H = grad²ℓ. For logistic regression the Hessian is H = −Σ h⁽ⁱ⁾(1 − h⁽ⁱ⁾) x⁽ⁱ⁾(x⁽ⁱ⁾)ᵀ: negative definite, a weighted Gram matrix with weights h(1 − h). The update is iteratively reweighted least squares.

ℓ(θ) = −softplus(−θ). Gradient: y − g(θ). Second derivative: −g(θ)(1 − g(θ)).

From the lessonSL-05 §Correctness checks, §Failure case

Two checks. The Hessian must be negative definite for maximization: check its eigenvalues on a toy. On a quadratic, Newton converges in one step: test this on least squares. Two failure modes. n = 100,000 features: the Hessian is 1e10 entries and never fits in memory. Newton is the wrong tool. And on non-concave objectives Newton can jump to a minimum or a saddle. L-BFGS approximates the Hessian from gradient history with no explicit matrix.

The exponential family: one template, many models

From the lessonSL-06 §Source anchor, §Mental model

Linear regression and logistic regression look different but share the same update shape. What is the common template? Many distributions share one algebraic shape: p(y, η) = b(y) exp(ηᵀT(y) − a(η)). A base measure b(y), times exp of the natural parameter dot the sufficient statistic minus the log partition. The natural parameter η is the dial. The sufficient statistic T(y) is all the data the dial cares about. The log partition a(η) keeps it normalized.

Distributionη (natural parameter)T(y)a(η) (log partition)b(y)
Bernoullilog(φ/(1−φ)), the logitylog(1 + e^η)1
Gaussian (σ² = 1)μyη²/2(1/√(2π)) e^(−y²/2)

From the lessonSL-06 §Computed example, §Correctness checks

Bernoulli with φ = 0.7. η = log(0.7/0.3) = log(2.333) = 0.847. a(η) = log(1 + e^0.847) = log(3.333) = 1.204. Normalization check: Σ b(y) exp(ηy − a) = exp(−1.204) + exp(0.847 − 1.204) = 0.2997 + 0.6999 = 1.0. And the mean falls out of the log partition: da/dη = E[T(y)]. For Bernoulli: d/dη log(1 + e^η) = e^η/(1 + e^η) = φ. One caution from the notes page 32 footnote: with general σ² the Gaussian needs a two-dimensional η or a dispersion parameter. Using η = μ alone with unknown σ² is incomplete.

Unit 7 · SL-07 · C10, C11

The GLM recipe: invent a regression in five minutes

From the lessonSL-07 §Source anchor, §Mental model

Given a new target type, counts say, how do you invent the right regression in five minutes? Three steps. (1) Pick the exponential family that matches the target: Poisson for counts. (2) Set the prediction to the family mean: h(x) = E[y | x]. (3) Tie the natural parameter to the features linearly: η = θᵀx. The link function bridges the mean to the linear score: η = g_link(μ).

ModelFamilyLinkPrediction
Least squaresGaussianidentity: μ = ηh = θᵀx
Logistic regressionBernoullilogit: η = log(μ/(1−μ))h = g(θᵀx)
Poisson regressionPoissonlog: η = log μh = e^(θᵀx)

From the lessonSL-07 §Computed example

Customers per hour, y in {0, 1, 2, …}. Poisson: p(y, λ) = e^(−λ) λ^y / y!. Read off: η = log λ, T(y) = y, a(η) = e^η, b(y) = 1/y!. Mean: da/dη = e^η = λ. GLM: log μ = θᵀx, so h(x) = e^(θᵀx). Counts get a log link. Predictions stay positive automatically. Numbers: θ = [0.5, 0.2], x = [1, 3]. η = 0.5 + 0.6 = 1.1. Predicted mean: e^1.1 = 3.004 customers per hour.

From the lessonSL-07 §Correctness checks, §Failure case

The prediction must respect the target's range: positive for Poisson, (0, 1) for Bernoulli. The gradient keeps the (y − h)x shape for canonical links. This is the deeper reason the notes promised on page 24. The failure case: an identity link on counts. θᵀx goes negative and the model predicts −2 customers. The link exists to prevent exactly this.

The lesson

The link keeps predictions inside the target's world. An identity link on counts predicts −2 customers.

Why it matters. Range violations are silent. The model trains, the loss falls, and the output is nonsense the metric never checks. The link is a guardrail, not decoration.

Transfer. Every output has a valid range. Enforce the range in the architecture, not in post-processing.

Unit 8 · SL-08 · C12

Separation: when perfection breaks the math

From the lessonSL-08 §Source anchor, §Mental model

The data is perfectly separable. Logistic regression should be happy. Why do the weights blow up? If a line separates the classes perfectly, pushing the weights toward infinity keeps improving the likelihood. The sigmoid saturates toward 0/1 and the loss keeps falling. No finite maximum exists. The optimizer never stops. The weights grow without bound.

Illustration The divergence dial. The lesson's two points: (1, 0) label 0, (2, 1) label 1, with θ = [−1.5c, c]. Grow c and watch the loss fall toward zero while the weights grow without bound.

c: 1.0

Scores: −0.5c and 0.5c. Loss per point: softplus(−0.5c). Try c = 10: loss near 0.013, weights [−15, 10].

From the lessonSL-08 §Diagnosis and fixes

Diagnose: weights grow steadily, loss keeps falling, predictions saturate at 0/1. Fix: add a penalty (ridge, U09), which bounds the weights. Or use a Bayesian prior (U09). The perceptron has the same issue in reverse: it stops at the first separator with no margin preference. U06 fixes this with max-margin. Linear discriminant analysis (U04) always has a finite solution: it fits class densities, not a separating line.

6Nugget 6 · From the lesson SL-08 §Mental model
Nugget 6 · From the lesson SL-08 §Mental model
The lesson

Perfect data breaks maximum likelihood. When the classes separate cleanly, the finite optimum disappears and the weights run to infinity.

Why it matters. The best-case data produces the worst-case behavior. A model that trains forever on easy data is not learning. It drifts toward infinity, politely.

Transfer. Watch for success modes that destroy the objective's finite optimum. The fix is a penalty that bounds the answer, decided before the data arrives.

Unit 9 · Synthesis

The arc, end to end

From the lessonSL-01 to SL-08

The arc, end to end. The sigmoid squeezes any real score into a probability, and its gradient has the LMS-like shape (y − h)x with h nonlinear in θ (SL-01). The loss must never form the probability at the extremes: the softplus form is correctness, not optimization (SL-02). Softmax extends the story to k classes with the predicted-minus-true gradient (SL-03). The perceptron thresholds the same score, updates only on mistakes, and carries no probabilities (SL-04).

Newton fits a quadratic bowl at the current point and jumps to its top: fewer steps, costlier steps, and a Hessian that never fits in memory at n = 100,000 (SL-05). Beneath it all sits one template: the exponential family, where the sigmoid appears by algebra when Bernoulli's natural parameter inverts (SL-06). The GLM recipe turns any target type into a regression in three steps, and the link keeps predictions inside the target's world (SL-07). And the pathology: perfect separation removes the finite optimum, so the weights grow without bound unless a penalty bounds them (SL-08).

From the lesson§Research reading, §Nearest alternatives

Where the unit points next, in the lesson's own forward links. L-BFGS approximates the Hessian from gradient history with no explicit matrix. Linear discriminant analysis (U04) always has a finite solution on separable data. U06 fixes the perceptron's margin indifference with max-margin. U09 supplies the ridge penalty and the Bayesian prior that bound the weights. The extension: fit logistic regression two ways, gradient ascent and Newton, on a separable and a non-separable toy. Log weight norms and loss per iteration. The falsifiable claim: on separable data the gradient-ascent weight norm grows without bound while the loss tends to zero, and Newton diverges faster.
