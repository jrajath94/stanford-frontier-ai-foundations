Unit 1 · SL-01 · C01, C02

The objects of a learning problem

From the lessonSL-01

Every supervised problem has four objects. A feature is one measured input. The target is the answer to predict. One example is one row of features plus its target. The dimension is the count of features. Rows are examples. Columns are dimensions.

The lesson's motivating case is a hospital that predicts heart attacks. One patient: age 52, systolic 140, cholesterol 210. Outcome 1, a heart attack within ten years. Second patient: age 41, systolic 118, cholesterol 180. Outcome 0. Two examples, three dimensions. The feature vector x has shape (n,). Here n = 3. The target y is a scalar, 0 or 1. The design matrix X stacks the m examples and has shape (m, n). Here it is (2, 3).

From the lessonSL-01 §Failure case

One feature can poison the whole table. A feature that exists only after the target, such as the surgery report when the task is to predict a heart attack, leaks the answer. The model looks good on paper and is useless in the ward. Name this failure: target leakage.

And one distinction worth its own line. Some problems have no target column at all. That is unsupervised learning, not a broken supervised problem. The split sits in the task, not in the data format.

2 linesFrom the lesson SL-01 §Correctness check
Nugget 1 · From the lesson SL-01 §Correctness check
The lesson

Rows of X equal the length of y. Columns of X equal n. This invariant holds in all code in this course.

Why it matters. Shape bugs masquerade as model bugs. A model that trains on a misaligned table learns a real pattern in wrong data, and no loss curve flags it.

Transfer. Name the container, its rows, and its columns before any pipeline. The audit in code is two lines. The cost of skipping it is a week.

Unit 2 · SL-02 · C03

Hypothesis and parameters: the dials, not the rule

From the lessonSL-02

Two students fit a line through the same data and get different lines. What exactly did they choose? A hypothesis is a rule with dials. The rule is fixed: a line. The dials are parameters: the intercept and the slope. Learning turns the dials. The designer picks the rule itself, which is the hypothesis class.

The linear hypothesis reads h_θ(x) = θ₀ + θ₁x₁ + … + θₙxₙ, written h_θ(x) = θᵀx with x₀ = 1 folded in. The parameter vector θ has shape (n,), plus the intercept. The class is fixed before training. The parameters are the only freedom.

From the lessonSL-02 §Nearest alternative, §Failure case

Keep two changes apart. Changing dials stays inside the class. Changing the class itself, say fitting a curve instead of a line, changes which dials exist. Class choice is a design decision (model selection, U09). Parameter choice is a training decision.

Both extremes fail. A class with zero dials cannot learn. A class with more dials than examples memorizes. Opposite causes, same outcome.

Transfer. In any system, separate what is fixed from what moves. Interventions at the wrong layer waste the effort.

Unit 3 · SL-03 · C04

Three kinds of learning

From the lessonSL-03

When does a dataset have answers, and what changes when it does not? Supervised learning: examples carry targets, and the task is to predict the target from the features. Unsupervised learning: examples carry no targets, and the task is to find structure, such as clusters, densities, or low-dimensional views. Reinforcement learning: an agent acts, the world responds, reward arrives later, and the task is to choose actions that earn future reward.

The evaluation must change with the task. Supervised accuracy on ten held-out patients: 8 correct, 2 wrong, accuracy 0.8. Unsupervised on the same ten without labels: 2 clusters, sizes 6 and 4. No accuracy exists, because no labels exist.

From the lessonSL-03 §Nearest alternatives

Two hybrids bridge the gap. Self-supervised learning invents targets from the data itself, for example predicting a missing word. It is supervised machinery on unsupervised data, and U13 builds on it. Semi-supervised learning uses a few labels plus many unlabeled examples. In RL, one assumption carries weight: reward correlates with the true goal. U15 examines the cases where it does not.

3Nugget 3 · From the lesson SL-03 §Failure case
Nugget 3 · From the lesson SL-03 §Failure case
The lesson

No labels, no accuracy. Treating an unsupervised task with supervised metrics invents fake accuracy.

Why it matters. Clustering has no correct label order, so accuracy against arbitrary cluster names is meaningless. The metric reports a number and the number says nothing.

Transfer. Metrics follow the task. A metric without a task is a costume. When the task changes, retire the metric with it.

Unit 4 · SL-04 · C05

Two risks: the one you measure and the one you care about

From the lessonSL-04

Your model scores 98 percent on the data you trained on. What will it score on tomorrow's patients? Two risks answer two questions. Empirical risk is the average loss on the training set: R̂(h) = (1/m) Σ L(h(x⁽ⁱ⁾), y⁽ⁱ⁾). It is a count on data you own. Population risk is the average loss over all future data from the same source: R(h) = E[L(h(x), y)]. It is the quantity you actually care about. Training minimizes the first and hopes about the second.

The lesson's coin makes the gap concrete. Train on ten flips: 7 heads. A model that always says heads scores 0.70 on the training flips. The true chance of heads is 0.5. The training score lies upward. Why can the learner not just minimize population risk? It has no access to the expectation, only to m samples. The empirical risk is the sample estimate. Minimizing it is justified when the estimate is close to the truth, and generalization theory (U08) quantifies exactly when that holds.

From the lessonSL-04 §Correctness check, §Failure case

One audit rule. If empirical risk exceeds the fresh-data risk on a large sample, suspect a bug or a distribution shift. In the standard setting, empirical risk is the optimistic one. And one failure mode with a name: a model that memorizes the training set drives empirical risk to zero and population risk up. That is overfit. The mechanism is dial count. The class had enough dials to fit noise.

The lesson

The training score flatters. Empirical risk is the optimistic one, and the gap between the two risks is the central object of learning theory.

Why it matters. Every decision that trusts a training number trusts a biased witness. Stakeholders see 100 percent in training and 60 percent in production, and the surprise is avoidable.

Transfer. Any number measured on the data that tuned the system is optimistic. Gate real decisions on held-out numbers only. The lesson's own production rule: release gates read held-out metrics, never training metrics.

Unit 5 · SL-05 · C06

Losses: the steering wheel is not the scorecard

From the lessonSL-05

Why do we train on one number and report another? A loss scores one prediction. Squared loss (ŷ − y)² punishes large errors hard. Zero-one loss counts mistakes. The loss is the training steering wheel. The metric is the business scorecard. They often differ: you train with squared loss and report median error.

The lesson's house price toy: true 400k, predicted 350k. Squared loss: (50,000)² = 2,500,000,000. Absolute loss: 50,000. Zero-one loss is not defined for regression. It needs a right-or-wrong verdict. For binary targets with predicted probability 0.8 and true label 1, log loss is −log(0.8) = 0.2231. At predicted 0.2 and true 1: −log(0.2) = 1.6094. The loss grows as confidence in the wrong answer grows.

From the lessonSL-05 §Correctness check, §Failure case

Two audit lines. Loss is a scalar. It must be lower for better predictions on fixed data. If a loss goes up when predictions improve, the sign is wrong. And squared loss on data with a recording error of ten million dollars lets one bad row dominate the fit. Heavy-tail losses or data cleaning are the fix (U09).

One design option. Use the business metric directly as the loss when it is smooth enough to optimize. Use a smooth proxy loss when the metric has flat pieces (see diagnostic D08).

Nugget 5 · From the lesson SL-05 §Mental model
The lesson

The loss steers. The metric scores. Training on one and reporting another is normal, not a cheat.

Why it matters. A team that confuses the two tunes the steering wheel to please the scorecard. The model then optimizes a number nobody ships.

Transfer. Keep the steering signal and the score signal in separate hands. State both at the start of every project.

Unit 6 · SL-06 · C07

Train and test: the contract

From the lessonSL-06

When is it legal to trust the test score? The contract has two clauses. Training data and future data come from the same distribution. Examples do not influence each other: IID, independent and identically distributed. Break the contract and every guarantee in U08 voids.

The split has three jobs. Train fits. Validation selects. Test reports once. One rule binds all three: no leakage from test into train.

From the lessonSL-06 §Computed example, §Failure case

Two classic breaches. A time series of stock prices by day: a random split puts future days in training and past days in test. The model peeks at the future. The proper split trains on early days and tests on later days. The random-split score is a lie. Second breach: a patient appears in both train and test through duplicate records. The test score measures memory, not learning. Fix: dedupe by patient id before splitting.

6Nugget 6 · From the lesson SL-06 §Nearest alternative
Nugget 6 · From the lesson SL-06 §Nearest alternative
The lesson

When IID fails by design, split by the failure unit: time blocks, hospitals, users.

Why it matters. A random split on time-ordered data is not a neutral choice. It is a leak that always flatters. The test must resemble deployment, not the spreadsheet.

Transfer. Every evaluation has a unit that can break it. Find that unit and split on it. The hospital toy in Section 1 deploys per hospital, so it splits per hospital.

Unit 7 · SL-07 · C10

Optimization is not generalization

From the lessonSL-07

Your optimizer found the lowest possible training cost. Why might the model still be bad? Two questions, two fields. Optimization asks: how low can the cost go on this data? Generalization asks: how low will the risk be on new data? Optimization is a computation problem. Generalization is a statistics problem. A perfect optimizer can still generalize poorly.

From the lessonSL-07 §Nearest alternative, §Failure case

One remedy with a name: early stopping (U09) halts optimization before it harms generalization. It trades a higher training cost for a lower test error. And one misdiagnosis to avoid: blaming the optimizer for a generalization failure. Tuning the learning rate will not fix a hypothesis class that is too rich. Diagnose which of the two problems you have first.

2 differentproblems with two different causes of failure · From the lesson SL-07 §Mental model
Nugget 7 · From the lesson SL-07 §Mental model
The lesson

Low training cost and low future risk are two different problems with two different causes of failure.

Why it matters. A team that tunes hyperparameters against a generalization failure burns weeks. The dials were never the layer that failed.

Transfer. Name the problem before the fix. Is the number bad because the search failed, or because the winner cannot travel?

Unit 8 · SL-08 · C08, C09

Shapes: notation as compressed code

From the lessonSL-08

How do you read θᵀXᵀXθ without fear? A vector is a list with a direction. A matrix is a table. The shape is the contract: it tells you what can multiply what. Notation is compressed code. Expand it once and it stops being scary.

The lesson's computed example: X is (2, 2), θ is (2,). Then Xθ = [1×1 + 2×(−1), 3×1 + 4×(−1)] = [−1, −1], shape (2,). XᵀX has shape (2, 2). Xᵀy has shape (2,). The normal equations (XᵀX)θ = Xᵀy read (2,2)(2,) = (2,). Shapes balance on both sides.

import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1.0, 2.0], [3.0, 4.0]])
y = np.array([5.0, 11.0])
theta = np.array([1.0, 2.0])

pred = X @ theta            # shape (2,)
assert pred.shape == (2,)
gram = X.T @ X              # shape (2, 2)
assert gram.shape == (2, 2)
rhs = X.T @ y               # shape (2,)
assert rhs.shape == (2,)

From the lessonSL-08 §Objects, §Failure case

Two readings to keep apart. x⁽ⁱ⁾ is example i. xᵢ is feature i of one vector. In this course the superscript in parentheses is always the example index. A power is written without parentheses. And the audit rule from Section 1 returns, one level up: a (m, n) times a (n,) gives a (m,). A (n, m) times a (m,) gives a (n,). When shapes do not balance, the formula is wrong or a transpose is absent.

Unit 9 · SL-09 · C11, C12

Baselines and measurable tasks

From the lessonSL-09

Before the first model, what do you write down? A baseline is the dumbest method that still uses the data: the mean for regression, the majority class for classification. It sets the bar. A measurable task states the data, the metric, and the decision rule: what ships if the metric hits the target.

The lesson's house price case: mean of training prices 520k. Baseline prediction: 520k for every house. Mean absolute error of the baseline: 85k. Any model must beat 85k MAE on the test set to earn its place. A domain heuristic can serve as the baseline instead of a statistical one, for example predicting the last known price. Choose the strongest dumb method, not the weakest.

From the lessonSL-09 §Correctness check, §Failure case

If a fancy model cannot beat the mean, either the features carry no signal or the model is broken. Both are useful findings. And a task with no decision rule, for example exploring the data, cannot fail and cannot succeed. Measurable means: metric, threshold, and what happens at the threshold.

85kMAE is not a worse model · From the lesson SL-09 §Mental model
Nugget 8 · From the lesson SL-09 §Mental model
The lesson

Beat the mean first. The dumbest honest method is the bar every clever method must clear.

Why it matters. A model that cannot beat 85k MAE is not a worse model. It is evidence that the features carry no signal, which is a finding, not a failure.

Transfer. Always build the dumbest honest version first. It costs an hour and it prices every later claim.

Unit 10 · Synthesis

The arc, end to end

From the lessonSL-01 to SL-09

The arc, end to end. Every learning problem has objects: features, targets, examples, dimensions (SL-01). A hypothesis is a fixed rule with movable dials, and the class is the designer's choice while the dials are the trainer's (SL-02). Data comes with answers, without answers, or with delayed reward, and the metric must change with the task (SL-03).

Two risks frame the whole course. Empirical risk is the average loss on the training set. Population risk is the expectation over unseen data. Training minimizes the first and hopes about the second, and the gap between them is the central object of learning theory (SL-04). The loss steers training while the metric scores the business, and the two need not match (SL-05). The test score is legal only under the IID contract, and when the contract fails by design, the split follows the failure unit (SL-06).

Optimization and generalization are two problems with two failure causes (SL-07). Notation is compressed code, and shapes are the contract that catches formula errors before code runs (SL-08). Before the first model: a baseline, a metric, a threshold, and a decision rule (SL-09).
