C01 · Transport coupling

The cheapest plan is the distance

Two piles of mass: p on {0, 2} with 0.5 each, q on {1, 3} with 0.5 each. From the lesson C01 A coupling is a transport plan: gamma(i, j) says how much mass moves from the point i of p to the point j of q. Rows must sum to the masses of p, columns to the masses of q. The diagonal plan (0→1, 2→3) costs 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 1 = 1.0. The crossed plan (0→3, 2→1) costs 0.5 × 3 + 0.5 × 1 = 2.0. Both plans are legal. The distance takes the cheaper one.

Plangamma matrixCost
diagonal[[0.5, 0], [0, 0.5]]1.0
crossed[[0, 0.5], [0.5, 0]]2.0

W1(p, q) = min over gamma of sum of gamma(i,j) |x_i − y_j|. From the lesson C01 The min runs over all couplings. On the toy the min is 1.0. The cost matrix |x_i − y_j| = [[1, 3], [1, 1]]. The failure case: a gamma whose columns do not sum to q. It is not a coupling, and the cost it reports is meaningless. The marginal assert catches it. Nobody computes couplings on images (K^2 memory): the dual (C03) replaces the matrix with a function.

Nugget 01 · C01

The cheapest plan is the distance.

What happened
Two legal transport plans cost 1.0 and 2.0. The distance is the min, not either plan.
Why it matters
Without the minimization, coupling cost is a set of numbers, not a number. The min is what makes W1 a ruler instead of a menu.
Transferable principle
Optimize before you report. companion A quantity defined over choices is not a number until the best choice is taken.

C02 · Wasserstein distance

A gradient where JS gives none

P0 = delta_0, P_theta = delta_theta. The supports are disjoint for every theta except 0. From the lesson C02 KL is infinite. JS = 1 bit for every theta except 0, and 0 at theta = 0. The generator parameter moves, but the JS objective does not. Its gradient in theta is 0. Training is dead. W1 on point masses: the only coupling moves all mass from 0 to theta, so W1 = |theta|. No smoothing, no overlap needed.

The sweep over theta in [−2, 2]: From the lesson C02 W1 = |theta|, a straight V with slope ±1. JS = 1 bit flat, then a cliff to 0 at theta = 0. At theta = 1: W1 = 1.0 with dW1/dtheta = 1.0 (finite difference 1.0000000000287557). JS = 1 bit with dJS/dtheta = 0. The generator needs the 1.0. JS gives the 0.

Nugget 02 · C02

A gradient where JS gives none.

What happened
On disjoint supports JS sat at 1 bit with zero gradient while W1 traced |theta| with slope ±1.
Why it matters
The generator can only follow a signal that moves when it moves. A flat objective is not a neutral objective. It is a dead one.
Transferable principle
Pick the ruler that sees the motion. companion When the supports separate, the metric must live in the data space, not in bits.

C03 · Dual assumptions

The bound sets the scale

The Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual: From the lesson C03 W1(p, q) = max over f with L(f) ≤ 1 of E_p[f(x)] − E_q[f(x)]. The lesson reads it as a price story: f(x) is a price quoted at location x, a shipper buys mass at the prices of q and sells at the prices of p, and the 1-Lipschitz rule says prices at nearby points cannot differ by more than the distance: no arbitrage across space. The max profit of a fair shipper equals the cheapest transport cost.

Three critics at theta = 1: From the lesson C03 f(x) = −x (1-Lipschitz) gives gap 1.0 = W1. f(x) = −2x (breaks the bound) gives 2.0, an overestimate. The linear critic with |w| ≤ 0.01 (the weight clip of C05) gives 0.01, an underestimate. Drop the Lipschitz rule and maximize over all f: f(x) = −100x gives 100.0. Without the bound the formula is not a distance.

The three assumptions, each load-bearing: From the lesson C03 (1) the ground cost is a metric, here |x − y|. (2) f is 1-Lipschitz on the whole space, not just at the samples. (3) the max runs over all such f, so the critic class must be rich enough. Break any one and the gap is not W1. The failure case for (2) is exactly the gradient-penalty blind spot of C06.

Nugget 03 · C03

The bound sets the scale.

What happened
Removing the 1-Lipschitz bound let the gap reach any number: 2.0, 100.0, unbounded. The bound is what makes the max a distance.
Why it matters
Every WGAN implementation is an attempt to keep this bound on a neural net. The bound is not a technicality. It is the entire content of the dual.
Transferable principle
A max without a constraint is not a measurement. companion The constraint is what turns an optimization into a ruler.

C04 · Lipschitz critic

Honest bounds are loose

A critic is a composition: f(x) = w2 relu(W1 x + b1) + b2. From the lesson C04 ReLU has constant 1. An affine map x → W x has constant equal to the largest singular value of W. So L(f) ≤ sv(w2) × sv(W1) × 1. On the critic toy: W1 = [[2], [−1]] (2-by-1), w2 = [[0.5, 1]] (1-by-2). sv(W1) = sqrt(5) = 2.2361. sv(w2) = sqrt(1.25) = 1.1180. Product = 2.5. Finite differences on a 20001-point grid give max slope 1.0, under 2.5. The bound holds and is not tight.

The lesson warns against reading the bound backward. From the lesson C04 The true constant can be smaller than the product, and scaling the gap by the bound does not recover W1. The bound is one-directional. An SVD per layer per step is O(min(m,n)^3): a diagnostic and a design tool, not a loop component.

Nugget 04 · C04

Honest bounds are loose.

What happened
The spectral product said 2.5. The true max slope was 1.0. The bound was correct and 2.5x loose.
Why it matters
Sufficient is not necessary. A loose bound cannot calibrate the gap, and dividing by it does not produce W1. The bound tells you what is safe, never what is true.
Transferable principle
Never read an upper bound as an equality. companion A guarantee in one direction is a trap in the other.

C05 · Weight clipping

The box is the disease

After each critic update, clip every weight into [−c, c] with c = 0.01. From the lesson C05 A linear critic f(x) = w x then has |w| ≤ 0.01, so it is 0.01-Lipschitz. The dual gap becomes 0.01 × theta at best. At theta = 1: true W1 = 1.0, clipped gap = 0.01. The number on the screen is 1 percent of the truth.

Two costs beyond the biased number: From the lesson C05 capacity collapse and scale collapse. Clip 2000 weights drawn from U[−0.1, 0.1] to [−0.01, 0.01]: 90.9 percent land exactly on the boundary (seed 0). The net loses its interior: almost every weight is −c or +c. Five layers of ±0.01 scale every output by 0.01^5 = 1e-10, and gradients through the stack shrink by the same product. The biased gap is the visible symptom. The starved function class is the disease.

clipped gap = 0.01 · true W1 = 1.0 · bias factor = 100x

Nugget 05 · C05

The box is the disease.

What happened
Clipping gave a gap of 0.01 on a true 1.0, and 90.9 percent of the weights ended glued to the box walls.
Why it matters
Scaling the gap back up by 1/c recovers the magnitude only if the clipped critic was optimal in its box. It usually is not: the box also cripples the class. The enforcement that was supposed to keep the critic honest ate the critic.
Transferable principle
Price the enforcement, not just the idea. companion A cheap constraint with a 100x bias is the most expensive line in the codebase.

C06 · Gradient penalty

A zero penalty is not a certificate

The penalty samples points on lines between real and fake samples: xhat = eps × x + (1 − eps) × y, eps ~ U[0, 1]. From the lesson C06 At each xhat it measures the gradient norm and adds lambda × (norm − 1)^2 to the critic loss. The critic learns slope 1 along the corridors where mass travels. Linear toy, lambda = 10: at |w| = 0.3 the two-sided penalty is 10 × 0.49 = 4.9 while the one-sided max(0, norm − 1)^2 form gives 0.0. At |w| = 1.0 both give 0.0. At |w| = 2.5 both give 22.5. The two-sided form punishes flat and steep alike. A critic with slope 0.3 everywhere pays nothing under the one-sided form, yet its gap underestimates W1 by 3x.

The blind spot: the penalty looks only on the interpolation segments. From the lesson C06 Off those segments it is blind. The counterexample: f(x) = x + 3 exp(−((x−5)/0.1)^2), a spike at x = 5. Interpolations between 0 and 1 give mean penalty 5.1e-22, essentially zero, while the max gradient near x = 5 is 26.7. The critic passes the penalty and still breaks the bound where nobody sampled. Treating a zero penalty as proof of 1-Lipschitz is the failure case. The penalty is a sample estimate on segments, not a certificate. It costs a second backward pass per critic step: roughly double the critic-step cost of the clip.

Bench. Move the slope and read both penalties. Illustration Lesson formulas with lambda = 10: two-sided 10(|w|−1)^2, one-sided 10 max(0, |w|−1)^2.


two-sided = 4.90 · one-sided = 0.00

Nugget 06 · C06

A zero penalty is not a certificate.

What happened
The penalty read 5.1e-22 on the sampled segments while a spike at x = 5 carried gradient 26.7. The critic passed the test and broke the bound.
Why it matters
The penalty is a sample estimate on segments, not a proof over the space. Every enforcement has a coverage, and the uncovered region is where the violation hides.
Transferable principle
Audit the coverage of the audit. companion A check that only looks where you point it will miss exactly what you did not point at.

C07 · Training stability

When the loss is a ruler

The critic maximizes E_p[f] − E_q[f]. The generator minimizes that max over theta. From the lesson C07 When the critic is near-optimal, the number on the screen approximates W1, and its gradient in theta is the direction that shrinks the distance. The loss is a ruler with a needle. At theta = 1: exact W1 = 1.0. Optimal linear critic (w = −1): gap 1.0, dW/dtheta = 1.0, the generator steps toward 0. Suboptimal critic (w = −0.5): gap 0.5, gradient 0.5, half the signal. JS at theta = 1: 1 bit, gradient 0.

The diagnostic that keeps it honest: the calibration ratio, gap / W_exact on a reference pair where W_exact is known. From the lesson C07 Ratio near 1: the critic tracks, and the loss means distance. Ratio near 0.5: the critic lags. Ratio above 1 on the toy means a bug: no 1-Lipschitz critic can beat W1. The failure case: a falling critic loss with a frozen generator. The critic found a better witness, the distance did not move. Celebrating the loss drop as progress mistakes the ruler for the engine.

C08 · Critic frequency

The frequency is a budget decision

The game is min over theta, max over f of the gap. From the lesson C08 After each generator move the old argmax critic is stale, so n_critic steps of critic training re-approach the max before the next generator step. Toy at theta = 1, exact W1 = 1.0: a critic trained to w = −0.5 (few steps) gives gap 0.5, half the distance, half the gradient. A critic trained to w = −1 (more steps) gives gap 1.0, the full signal. The authored reference loop uses n_critic = 5, an authored choice for the toy.

Authored budget arithmetic: critic step 2 units, generator step 3 units. From the lesson C08 n_critic = 1 costs 5 units per generator step, 5 costs 13, 10 costs 23. The critic dominates the bill at n_critic = 5: 10 of 13 units. Raise n_critic until the calibration ratio saturates near 1, then stop: more steps past saturation buy nothing. The failure case: n_critic = 100 with the clip enforcement. The critic overfits its box, the gap looks optimal, and the generator still gets a biased signal. Frequency cannot fix a broken enforcement.

C09 · Applications

Three prices for the same inverse

Three applications share one trick: an adversary shapes a representation. From the lesson C09 First, GAN inversion: z* = argmin over z of ||G(z) − x||^2 + reg(z). On the linear toy G(z) = A z with A = [[1], [2]], x = [1.5, 3.0]: the normal equations give z* = (A^T A)^−1 A^T x = 7.5 / 5 = 1.5, residual 1.1e-16. Second, latent regression: train an encoder E on pairs (G(z), z). On the toy E(x) = x1/5 + 2x2/5, and E([1.5, 3.0]) = 0.3 + 1.2 = 1.5. One forward pass replaces the per-x optimization. Third, domain adversarial nets: the feature extractor gets the task gradient minus lambda times the domain gradient. On the scalar toy the domain gradient 0.5 becomes −0.5 at lambda = 1.

Each application assumes something. From the lesson C09 Inversion assumes G is near-injective: with G(z) = z^2 and x = 4, the argmin has two answers, 2 and −2, and no tiebreaker. Latent regression assumes the training pairs cover the x you will invert. Domain adversarial training assumes the domain label is the nuisance to remove, not signal the task needs. The failure case: invert an x far from the range of G. The residual stays large and z* is the nearest manifold point, not the code that made x. Report z* without the residual and the miss is hidden.

C11 · Limitations

The number you optimize is not the number you want

Six derived limitations, each with its mechanism. From the lesson C11 One: the gap is a lower bound. On the mode-drop toy (p_data = 0.5 N(−2,1) + 0.5 N(2,1), p_g = N(2,1)): exact W1 = 2.0 by the CDF integral, but a weak linear critic with |w| ≤ 0.01 reports gap 0.02. The distance sees the dropped mode. The critic misses it by 100x. Two: the clip biases the number (C05). Three: the penalty is blind off the segments (C06). Four: W1 lives in the ground metric, which in pixel space is not perceptual quality. Five: no mode-coverage guarantee, because training minimizes the critic's gap, not the exact W1. Six: the critic bill, n_critic steps plus a second backward pass for the penalty.

The 0.02 vs 2.0 pair is the whole lesson in one line. companion The guard is the calibration ratio (C07): no ratio, no claim. The failure case: the belief that WGAN cannot mode-collapse because W1 sees all mass. The exact W1 sees it. The trained system optimizes the gap. Conflating the two is the failure.

Nugget 07 · C11

The number you optimize is not the number you want.

What happened
Training minimized a critic gap of 0.02 while the true distance was 2.0. The optimizer did its job. The number it was given was 100x wrong.
Why it matters
Every limitation in the unit is a version of this split: the exact quantity you want against the estimated quantity you optimize. The calibration ratio is the only bridge between them.
Transferable principle
Distinguish the target from the proxy. companion Optimizing a proxy is fine. Believing the proxy is the target is not.

C12 · Fair comparison

Compare methods, not budgets

Compare three methods on the toy: vanilla GAN (JS), WGAN with the clip, WGAN with the gradient penalty. From the lesson C12 Fix the generator and critic architectures, the seed list (same seeds for all methods), the wall-clock or flop budget (not the step count), the critic frequency within each method's standard range, and the metrics (gap with calibration, coverage, sample quality). Vary only the loss and its enforcement. Report all seeds, not the best. Authored budget arithmetic: vanilla k = 1 costs 5 units per G step, WGAN n_critic = 5 costs 13, n_critic = 10 costs 23. Over 10,000 G steps the WGAN spends 130,000 units against 50,000. Declaring the WGAN better at 2.6x the compute is a budget comparison wearing a method comparison's clothes. Three methods × five seeds × 10k steps × 13 units = 1.95M units for the WGAN arm.

The failure case: best-of-5 seeds for the new method versus 1 seed for the baseline. From the lesson C12 The selection bias alone can flip a close race. Same seeds or no claim.
