Unit1

Unit 1

Eight files, one question

The lesson opens with eight files on disk. From the lesson C01 The patterns and counts: 0000 appears 3 times, 1111 appears 2 times, 0101 appears 2 times, 1010 appears 1 time. Nobody wrote a formula for them. They came from some unknown law of the world. The question that drives the whole unit: what exactly are we fitting when we train a generator?

This companion walks the twelve sections of the lesson in order. It keeps the same toy throughout, because the lesson does. Each chart below re-plots a number the lesson computes, with the section beside it.

Unit2

Unit 2 · Data versus model distribution

Three objects, three roles

The lesson names three objects and gives each a job. From the lesson C01 p_data is the unknown truth, the law that made the files. p_hat is the dataset seen as a distribution: each count divided by 8. q is the model, a formula with knobs. Training turns the knobs so q imitates p_data. The data never changes. The model does.

PatternCountp_hat = count/8q_uni = 1/4 each
000030.3750.25

The first attempt: memorize. Set q equal to p_hat and the model reproduces the counts exactly. From the lesson C01 It breaks with numbers. Eight files are too few to trust. The true law might give 1010 with probability 0.3, and we just saw it once by luck. The memorizer claims 1010 has probability 0.125 with total confidence. Memorization confuses the observed pile with the law that made it.

Nugget 01 · C01
The lesson

Name the three objects, then train.

What happened. The lesson splits the world into p_data, p_hat, and q, and allows only q to change.

Why it matters. Without the split, a model that copies the training counts looks perfect. With it, the copy is visibly a different claim about a different object.

Transferable principle. Before any fit, name which objects are fixed and which one is allowed to move. Confusion about what changed is the root of most broken evaluations.

Unit 3 · Density versus sample

The map and the raindrop

p_hat says the probability of 0000 is 0.375. That number is a claim about odds. The file 0000 sitting on disk is a fact. From the lesson C02 The claim is the density (mass, for discrete outcomes). The file is the sample. The lesson puts it as a picture: the density is the whole weather map, a sample is one raindrop. You cannot read the map from one drop.

The first attempt: estimate the law from one sample. The first file is 1111, so declare the probability of 1111 to be 1. It breaks with numbers. Seven of the eight files contradict the claim. One sample carries almost no information about the law.

Nugget 02 · C02
The lesson

One draw is not the map.

What happened. A single sample 1111 supports the claim that 1111 has probability 1, until the other seven files arrive.

Why it matters. Generative models are judged on the law they claim, not on any one file they emit. A beautiful sample proves nothing about the density.

Transferable principle. Judge the table, not the draw. Any single output is anecdote. The distribution is the claim.

UNIT 03
Unit4

Unit 4 · Likelihood

The bet on the whole dataset

Model q_uni says each file has probability 1/4. The dataset has eight files. From the lesson C03 If the files are independent draws, the probability of seeing exactly this dataset under the model is the product of the eight probabilities: (1/4)^8 = 1/65536 = 0.0000153. That is the likelihood. It is the model's bet on the observed data. High likelihood means the model finds the data unsurprising.

The raw product is unusable. With n = 1000 files the product has 600 digits, no computer stores it as a normal float, and every model scores zero. From the lesson C03 The fix: take the log. The log of a product is a sum. Log is base 2, so the unit is bits. Maximizing the likelihood is the same as maximizing the log-likelihood, because log rises everywhere.

ModelLog-likelihood of the 8 files (bits)Source
q_uni8 × (−2) = −16.0C03
q_emp (the memorizer)3×log(3/8) + 2×log(2/8) + 2×log(2/8) + 1×log(1/8) = −15.2451C03

The memorizer scores higher: −15.2451 beats −16.0. From the lesson C03 On training data it always does. That is the warning from C01, now with a number.

3Nugget 03 · C03
Nugget 03 · C03
The lesson

Take the log before the product dies.

What happened. A correct idea (multiply the probabilities) failed in arithmetic (the product underflows to zero), and a transform (the log) saved it.

Why it matters. The entire training objective of likelihood models is a sum of logs. The log step is not a trick. It is the only way the score survives contact with real data sizes.

Transferable principle. Change the scale, keep the idea. When a quantity underflows, a monotone transform preserves every comparison.

Unit5

Unit 5 · Support

The promise before the score

Consider q_bad: probability 1/2 on 0000, 1/2 on 1111, and zero on 0101 and 1010. From the lesson C04 The dataset contains 0101 twice and 1010 once. The model assigns those three files probability zero. The support is the set of outcomes the model admits exist. A log-likelihood that touches a zero becomes negative infinity. The model is infinitely surprised by data it already saw.

ModelSupportCovers the 8 files?Log-likelihood
q_uniall 4 patternsyes−16.0 bits
q_empall 4 patternsyes−15.2451 bits
q_bad0000, 1111 onlyno−infinity

The lesson draws the hard line: the model support must cover the data support. From the lesson C04 Every observed outcome needs positive model mass. This is a requirement, not a preference. The lesson also gives the repair: smoothed models add a tiny epsilon to every outcome, so the support is everything.

8 trainingfiles because three of them sat outside · Nugget 04 · C04
Nugget 04 · C04
The lesson

Zero mass on real data is fatal.

What happened. q_bad scored −infinity on the eight training files because three of them sat outside its support.

Why it matters. A NaN at step 0 of training is not a code bug. It is the support violation, reported honestly. The check that catches it costs O(K) and runs before any scoring.

Transferable principle. Check the promise before the score. Every scoring rule has a precondition. Run it first, always.

UNIT 05
Unit6

Unit 6 · Latent variables

The label you never see

A coin lands heads 55 times in 100 flips. From the lesson C05 One coin with bias 0.55 explains this. But a second story fits too: two coins in a bag, coin A lands heads with probability 0.9, coin B with probability 0.2, and we pick one at random each flip. The observed rate is still 0.55. One number, two stories. The stories differ in what they predict: the two-coin story predicts streaks, because a head suggests coin A, which makes the next head more likely.

The lesson names the hidden part z and writes the joint model p(x, z) = p(z) p(x | z). From the lesson C05 The prior p(z) says how often each hidden label occurs. The conditional p(x | z) says what each label produces.

zp(z)p(heads | z)p(tails | z)p(z, heads)p(z, tails)
A0.50.90.10.450.05
B0.50.20.80.100.40

The joint sums to 1. The failure case the lesson warns about: condition on z as if it were observed. The model then claims to know which coin was used on each flip, which the data never says.

5Nugget 05 · C05
Nugget 05 · C05
The lesson

Hidden causes need hidden labels.

What happened. Visible flips showed streaks a flat model could not state. A hidden label z (which coin) gave the model a language for the structure.

Why it matters. This is the seed of every latent-variable model in the course: VAEs (U05), discrete codes (U06), and the noise chain of diffusion (U07) all start by naming an unobserved cause.

Transferable principle. When the visible data shows structure a flat model cannot express, name the hidden part. The price is inference: z is hidden, so it must be summed out, which is C06.

UNIT 06
Unit7

Unit 7 · Marginalization

Removing what you cannot see

The joint table has four entries. The learner observes only heads and tails. From the lesson C06 The hidden label must go. The rule: weight by the prior, then sum. p(heads) = 0.5 × 0.9 + 0.5 × 0.2 = 0.45 + 0.10 = 0.55. p(tails) = 0.05 + 0.40 = 0.45.

The naive alternative averages the two conditional rates: (0.9 + 0.2) / 2 = 0.55. From the lesson C06 That happens to be right here only because the prior is 1/2 each. Change the prior to p(A) = 0.9 and the naive average still says 0.55 while the correct total is 0.9 × 0.9 + 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.83. The naive average ignores how often each label occurs.

Bayes flips the question. After seeing heads, which coin was it? From the lesson C06 p(A | heads) = 0.45 / 0.55 = 0.8182.

Unit8

Unit 8 · Entropy

How much uncertainty fits in a table

p_hat gives 0000 with probability 0.375. If you must guess the next file, how surprised will you be on average? From the lesson C07 Surprisal is −log p(x), the bits needed to name one draw. A rare outcome surprises more. Entropy is the average surprisal: H(p) = sum of p(x) × (−log p(x)) over all x.

Outcomep_hat−log p_hat (bits)Contribution
00000.3751.41500.5306

01010.252.00.5

Total H(p_hat)1.9056 bits

The lesson sets two anchors. From the lesson C07 A uniform law over 4 outcomes has entropy log(4) = 2 bits. A law with all mass on one outcome has entropy 0. Our 1.9056 sits just below 2, because p_hat is close to uniform. And the failure case: compute the entropy of the raw counts (3, 2, 2, 1) without dividing by n and the result is meaningless. Always normalize first.

Unit9

Unit 9 · Cross-entropy

The price of believing the wrong model

The data comes from p_hat. The model q_uni claims 1/4 everywhere. From the lesson C08 Score the model by the data: for each outcome take the model surprisal −log q(x), and average it with the data odds p_hat(x). Cross-entropy is the price of believing q while the world runs p. If q matches p, the price equals the entropy. If q is wrong, the price is higher.

The wrong first attempt: average the surprisal of the model with the odds of the model. That is just the entropy of the model, 2 bits. It never looks at the data. From the lesson C08 The score must weight by p, the data, or it measures nothing about fit.

Here log(0.25) = −2 for every term, so From the lesson C08 H(p_hat, q_uni) = 0.375×2 + 0.25×2 + 0.25×2 + 0.125×2 = 2.0 bits. Check: 2.0 is above the entropy 1.9056. Cross-entropy never beats the entropy of p. The gap is the subject of C09.

Cross-entropy is not symmetric. From the lesson C08 The lesson computes H(q_uni, q_emp) = 2.1038 bits, not 2.0. Swapping the roles changes the number.

Unit10

Unit 10 · KL divergence

The surcharge, itemized

The data needs 1.9056 bits per file under its own odds. Believing q_uni costs 2.0 bits per file. From the lesson C09 The difference, 0.0944 bits, is the price of the wrong model. KL divergence is the surcharge: cross-entropy is the total bill, entropy is the unavoidable part, KL is the part the model choice caused. D_KL(p || q) = H(p, q) − H(p).

Term by term with the log ratio: From the lesson C09 0.375×log(0.375/0.25) + 0 + 0 + 0.125×log(0.125/0.25) = 0.2194 − 0.125 = 0.0944 bits. It matches the subtraction: 2.0 − 1.9056 = 0.0944.

D_KL(p_hat || model) = 0.0944 bits · TV(p_hat, model) = 0.125

6Nugget 06 · C09
Nugget 06 · C09
The lesson

Subtract the noise, keep the fault.

What happened. Cross-entropy 2.0 mixed two things: the noise of the data itself (1.9056) and the fault of the model (0.0944). Subtraction isolated the fault.

Why it matters. Two datasets can give the same model the same cross-entropy for entirely different reasons: noisy data, or a bad model. The raw bill does not separate the two. KL does.

Transferable principle. Isolate the part your choice caused. Any score that mixes baseline noise with decision fault will mislead you about which decisions helped.

Unit 11 · Divergence choice

No ruler is neutral

KL gave q_uni a surcharge of 0.0944 bits over p_hat. Another ruler is total variation: half the L1 distance. From the lesson C10 KL asks how much extra coding cost. TV asks how much mass must move to turn one table into the other. Different questions, different rulers.

Under q_bad, KL is infinite. TV is finite: From the lesson C10 TV(p_hat, q_bad) = (1/2)(0.125 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.125) = 0.375. When a model misses support, KL refuses to rank it at all while TV still gives a number.

Rulerq_uni vs p_hatq_bad vs p_hatBehavior on support miss
KL (bits)0.0944infinityrefuses, infinite
TV0.1250.375finite, capped at 1

The lesson states the selection boundary plainly. From the lesson C10 KL when you train by likelihood and need gradients that punish zero mass hard. Bounded or geometric divergences when models miss support during training. U02, U03, and U04 build these rulers in full.

Unit12

Unit 12 · Estimation

The data picks the parameter

Ten coin flips land heads 7 times. The model is one coin with unknown bias theta. From the lesson C11 The maximum likelihood estimator picks the theta that maximizes the log-likelihood of the observed data. At 0.5 the log-likelihood is −10.0 bits. At 0.7 it is 7×log(0.7) + 3×log(0.3) = −3.6021 − 5.2109 = −8.8129 bits. The data prefers 0.7 by 1.19 bits.

Is there a formula, or must we search? Differentiate the log-likelihood and set it to zero: From the lesson C11 dl/d theta = k/theta − (n−k)/(1−theta) = 0 gives theta = k/n. For k = 7, n = 10: theta = 0.7. The estimate is the observed frequency. This is not a coincidence to memorize. It falls out of the derivative.

The failure case: one flip lands heads. From the lesson C11 MLE says theta = 1: a certain coin from one observation. The estimator is unbiased in the long run but absurd on tiny data. The lesson selection boundary: MLE when data is plentiful, a prior-based estimate when n is tiny and a prior carries real knowledge.

Unit13

Unit 13 · Evaluation

The honest exam

q_emp memorized the eight training files and won the training log-likelihood (−15.2451 vs −16.0). From the lesson C12 Declaring it the winner is not evidence. It is the definition of fitting. A model that copies the answer sheet passes its own exam.

The protocol: held-out data, fresh files the model never trained on. From the lesson C12 Four new files arrive: 0000 twice, 1111 once, 0101 once. Mean log-mass per held-out file: q_uni scores −2.0 bits. q_emp scores [2×log(3/8) + log(2/8) + log(2/8)] / 4 = −6.8301 / 4 = −1.7075 bits.

ModelTrain mean LL (bits)Held-out mean LL (bits)
q_uni−2.0−2.0

q_emp still wins here, because the held-out files happen to follow the training pattern. From the lesson C12 The protocol is the point, not the winner: on different held-out data the memorizer can lose. A second protocol: sample. Draw 8 files from the model and inspect them. Log-likelihood tests the probabilities, sampling tests the output directly. Both matter.

8 wonNugget 07 · C12
Nugget 07 · C12
The lesson

The memorizer always wins its own exam.

What happened. The model that copied the training eight won the training score by construction, −15.2451 against −16.0.

Why it matters. The training score cannot detect memorization. Only data the model never saw can. The lesson one-line trade: rigor costs one extra dataset split and gains a score that cannot be gamed by memorization.

Transferable principle. Judge on data the model never saw. Any evaluation that reuses the training data measures memory, not knowledge.
