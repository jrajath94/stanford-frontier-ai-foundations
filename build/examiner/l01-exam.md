## Q3 [STANDARD] Cobb-Douglas, intuitive vs formal
Q: State the guest's version of Cobb-Douglas and the textbook's formal version, and give labor's share of output.
A: The guest's intuitive version: GDP growth = change in labor + change in capital + change in technology (additive), where labor is more workers or more productive hours, capital is buildings, plants, equipment, invested money, and technology is ideas that make labor more productive. The formal textbook version is multiplicative: output = A x K^a x L^b, where A is technology, K is capital, L is labor, and the exponents are each input's share of output. In growth rates: GDP growth = tech growth + a x (capital growth) + b x (labor growth). Douglas and Cobb published it in 1928. The formal form agrees with the guest's point: labor's contribution is its share of output, about 0.6 in the US, times how fast the workforce grows.
Location: "The growth equation" (L218).
Score: 1 — both forms, the variable definitions, the 1928 date, and the 0.6 share are in the lesson.

## Q4 [WALK-ME-THROUGH] The 3 percent toy economy
Q: Walk me through the 3 percent toy economy step by step, including the compounding, and state exactly what the $650 billion is a bid on.
A: Suppose an economy grows 3 percent a year, split evenly: 1 point from labor, 1 from capital, 1 from technology. The labor point is capped by demography: no investment decision made this quarter changes how many 20-year-olds exist. Now suppose an investment could buy 0.5 points of labor growth the way capital buys its point. Total growth moves from 3 percent to 3.5 percent, and the buyer captures the surplus. Compounded over a decade: 1.03^10 = 1.34; 1.035^10 = 1.41; ratio 1.41 / 1.34 = 1.05. That half point is the difference between $1.34 and $1.41 on every dollar of GDP: a 5 percent larger economy. The $650 billion is a bid on that half point — not on chatbots.
Location: "the toy version, with numbers" (L257-280).
Score: 1 — the split, the demography cap, the full arithmetic, and the "bid on that half point" conclusion are all in the lesson.

## Q6 [WALK-ME-THROUGH] The digital-labor mechanism
Q: Define digital labor precisely, then walk through the mechanism by which an agent doing a task becomes "labor produced by an investment decision."
A: Digital labor is the first labor force in history whose size changes through an investment decision instead of a birth rate. The mechanism has three steps. Step one: a task exists that a human would do for wages. Step two: an agent does it, consuming tokens. A token is the basic unit of text an AI model reads and writes: roughly three quarters of a word. Step three: every token an agent consumes costs compute, power, and buildings, all bought with capital. The wage becomes a CapEx line. The labor term moves when money moves, on a quarterly horizon, like capital always did. That is the whole mechanism. Nothing in it requires the agent to be as good as the human. It requires the agent to be cheap enough per task.

## Q14 [WALK-ME-THROUGH] The power path
Q: Walk me through the power path from the substation to the chip. Name each stage, give the voltage changes, and state the five-nines sizing rule.
A: Power arrives at the substation at high voltage. Power distribution centers (small buildings with white roofs) take it at medium voltage, 34.5 kV (34,500 volts), and distribute it onward to the rows of equipment. Transformers (the electrical kind, not the machine-learning kind) step voltage down: here from 34.5 kV to 480 or 415 volts, the level the data hall equipment takes. Each stage trades voltage for current at roughly constant power, until the level is safe for the racks. Medium voltage and low voltage switchgear sit between substation and rack: the industrial breaker system that routes circuits and protects them, cutting power when a fault would damage equipment — the home electrical panel built at the scale of a city, in one giant electrical room. The UPS (uninterruptible power supply) is a battery system that smooths the power flowing from the substation to the chip and covers the seconds between a grid failure and the generators starting, so a flicker never becomes an outage. Diesel generators are the last resort: engines that burn diesel when the grid is down, backing up the core network and storage, not the whole campus. Five nines means 99.999% uptime: about five minutes of downtime a year. The guest's rule: not every system needs it, but storage and networking do, so that in a full grid outage the team can still reach a checkpoint and move a workload. Backup is sized to what must survive, not to everything. Remote power panels are the breaker panels inside the data hall that feed the racks: the tail end of the power path, the last distribution and protection point before the racks draw their load.
Location: power-path subchapters: distribution centers (L690), transformers (L699), switchgear (L708), UPS (L718), diesel generators (L726), "the five-nines sizing rule" (L733); "remote power panels" (L836).

## Q15 [WALK-ME-THROUGH] The cooling path and methods
Q: Walk me through the cooling path and the three cooling methods: their rack-power bands, worked numbers, and the decision rule.
A: Chips turn almost all their power into heat. The path: air-cooled chillers (machines that make cold water, built from wound copper coils) push cold water through a chilled water loop; cooling distribution units (CDUs) inside the data center take water from the chilled water pipe and distribute it to the individual racks of GPUs; cold water enters the rack, a thermal transfer event moves heat from the energized chip into the water, hot water returns to the chillers where fans blow air over the copper coils and exhaust the heat, and cold water goes back to the racks. The loop recirculates. Three methods by rack power: air stays practical up to roughly 15-25 kW per rack; above that, fans cannot move enough air to keep up (air hits a wall around 20-50 kW per rack). Direct-to-chip liquid cooling bolts a cold plate directly onto each GPU and flows coolant through the plate: handles 100-150 kW per rack, the band where an Nvidia GB200 NVL72 rack, about 120 kW, lives. NVL72 is Nvidia's rack-scale design: 72 GPUs on one NVLink (Nvidia's high-speed GPU-to-GPU interconnect) domain. PUE runs roughly 1.05-1.15 against 1.4-1.8 for air. The physics: water conducts heat roughly 25 times better than still air. The price: plumbing at every server, and CDUs sized for the higher heat flux. By 2026 this was the dominant liquid method, about 55% of liquid-cooling deployments (Schneider Electric, via Data Center Knowledge, 2026). Immersion cooling, above about 175-200 kW per rack: servers sit in tanks of dielectric fluid, in two-phase designs boiling off the chip surface; 200-250+ kW per rack demonstrated (BitFury/Allied Control reached 250 kW per enclosure); PUE as low as 1.02. The decision rule: air below about 20 kW per rack, direct-to-chip for the 20-150 kW AI band, immersion only when the rack passes about 175 kW.
Location: "chillers and the chilled water loop" (L750); "CDUs" (L762); "direct-to-chip liquid cooling" (L769); "immersion cooling" (L791); the rack-power table (L810); PUE definition is not separately given, but PUE values are stated as ratios in the table.

## Q22 [FOLLOW-UP on Q21]
Q: Now break the thesis. Run the downside rows and state the sharp falsifier in one number.
A: Hold the $60M stack fixed and vary the revenue: $30M per MW per year (rent + services, optimistic) -> 2-year payback, thesis sings; $15M (chip rental only) -> 4 years, the session's base case; $10M (token prices fall a third) -> 6 years, depreciation race gets tight; $7.5M (token prices halve) -> 8 years, needs longer asset life than the books allow. ## Q27 [STANDARD] The moving bottleneck
Q: Name the moving-bottleneck sequence of the last four years, the evidence for each phase, and the decision rule.
A: A binding constraint is the one input that gates output right now: more of everything else changes nothing until it is fixed. Four years, three gates: chips, then memory, now energized shells, with skilled labor binding underneath the whole time. Chips, four years ago: in 2023, H100 lead times stretched to 36-52 weeks, and a single H100 card retailed for about $30,000 (HPCwire, August 2023). Training runs competed for scarce GPUs, and allocation was the strategy. By early 2024 lead times had eased to 3-4 months (Tom's Hardware), and buyers were reselling surplus cards. The session's decision rule for that era: when lead times run past two quarters, chips gate everything, and you buy allocation, not architecture. Memory, then: high-bandwidth memory and the memory subsystem of the chip package gated how many training clusters could ship. A 12-layer HBM3E stack rose from about $300 to about $500 on contract renewal (SEdaily, 2025), and by September 2026 spot buyers were paying about $2,100 for a 36GB HBM3E stack against $365-510 contract prices (Intuition Labs data, via financial press). SK hynix ran about 250% year to date on HBM scarcity (ainvest, May 2026), and Samsung's semiconductor division posted an 80% profit surge in Q3 2025 on HBM3E demand. Energized shells, today: the binding constraint is finding places where you can put the chips and turn them on — powered shells with the electrical and cooling plant ready. A power shell is an energized data center: the building, the substation, the cooling, everything needed so that you can plug in chips and start computing. Sometimes the binding layer is even more specific: individual components like switchgear (the industrial breakers that route and protect power circuits), chillers (the machines that make the cold water for cooling), or power generation equipment. Labor, always: the guest calls the shortage of tradespeople a bottleneck in its own right, with 9,000 people on site at Abilene every day against a town of 120,000 — electricians, welders, plumbers, construction workers. The session prices it exactly: $4.7M per MW of capitalized labor, so one gigawatt carries $4.7B in wages during construction, and Abilene's 2.1 GW is roughly $9.9B of labor CapEx. The decision rule: invest in whatever gates output this year, and be ready to move when the gate moves. Last year's gate is a sunk cost.

## Q31 [APPLIED-DESIGN] Cooling-method switch
Q: Applied design: your racks are moving from ~20 kW to ~120 kW each. What cooling method do you switch to, what changes in PUE, and what does it cost?

## Q32 [FOLLOW-UP on Q31]
Q: When would you go past direct-to-chip to immersion, and why did two-phase immersion adoption stall?
