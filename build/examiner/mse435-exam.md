## L01 — From Electrons to Tokens

### Q1 [MECHANISM] The 3 percent toy economy and the $650B bid
Q: Walk me through the 3 percent toy economy step by step, including the compounding math, and state exactly what the $650 billion is a bid on. Why is the labor term "the slow term"?
A: Split 3 percent annual growth into 1 point from labor, 1 from capital, 1 from technology. The labor point is capped by demography: no investment decision made this quarter changes how many 20-year-olds exist, and historically the labor term could only change through the birth rate, a 20-year lead time. Now suppose an investment could buy 0.5 points of labor growth the way capital buys its point. Total growth moves from 3 percent to 3.5 percent. Compounded over a decade: 1.03^10 = 1.34; 1.035^10 = 1.41; ratio 1.41 / 1.34 = 1.05. That half point is the difference between $1.34 and $1.41 on every dollar of GDP: a 5 percent larger economy. The $650 billion is a bid on that half point, not on chatbots.

### Q2 [MECHANISM] The Abilene inversion: from tax credits to negative power prices to a 2.1 GW campus
Q: Walk the Abilene mechanism end to end. Why did production tax credits create negative power prices? What did Crusoe's inversion do about it? And why did the grid still cap the build at 1.2 GW?
A: West Texas is consistently windy and sunny. Production tax credits paid renewable developers per clean electron produced, independent of the sale price. Developers got paid by the government to produce, and had to sell independent of the price. Crusoe inverted the usual plan: instead of moving energy to the computers, move the computers to the energy, because moving data over fiber is cheap while moving electrons over transmission lines is expensive and slow to permit. Crusoe signed its first two buildings in June 2024. The campus became 2.1 GW aggregate with a 1 GW substation (the largest privately owned substation in the US) plus a 200 MW substation, eight buildings serving Oracle and OpenAI (Project Stargate), a 350 MW gas plant, and one coherent cluster. But in March 2026 OpenAI and Oracle stopped expanding beyond 1.2 GW, citing power grid delays of over a year. 2.1 GW planned, 1.2 GW delivered, 57 percent of the plan. The lesson in one number: power availability, not chips, set the ceiling. 43 percent of the plan died in grid delays.

### Q5 [DESIGN] Choose the cooling method for a rack from its power draw
Q: Given a rack's power draw, which cooling method applies, and why? Walk the physics and the PUE ladder.
A: Air cooling stays practical up to roughly 15-25 kW per rack. Above that, fans cannot move enough air to keep up. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling bolts a cold plate onto each GPU and handles 100-150 kW per rack, the band where an Nvidia GB200 NVL72 rack (about 120 kW; 72 GPUs on one NVLink domain) lives. PUE runs roughly 1.05-1.15 against 1.4-1.8 for air. The physics: water conducts heat roughly 25 times better than still air. Immersion cooling, above about 175-200 kW per rack, removes the air entirely: 200-250+ kW per rack demonstrated (BitFury/Allied Control reached 250 kW per enclosure), PUE as low as 1.02. Its price: tanks instead of racks, expensive fluid ($50-100 per gallon single-phase, $200+ two-phase fluorocarbons), greenfield-only retrofits, and two-phase adoption stalled on PFAS regulation, with a replacement fluid qualified in early 2026 and the outcome pending into 2027. Decision rule: air below about 20 kW per rack, direct-to-chip for the 20-150 kW AI band, immersion only when the rack passes about 175 kW. The lesson also notes cooling distribution units sit inside the data center bridging the chilled water loop to the racks, and hot aisle containment keeps exhaust separated from cold supply so chillers do not burn energy fixing the mix.
Location: "chillers and the chilled water loop" (L750); "direct-to-chip liquid cooling" (L774); "immersion cooling" (L791); "hot aisle containment" (L821).

### Q7 [MECHANISM] "Economic obsolescence precedes physical obsolescence by years"
Q: Explain that sentence. The chips do not wear out in three years — so what kills them?
A: The driver is not wear. Each new chip generation delivers sharply better compute-per-watt, and data centers face hard power ceilings: the campus has a fixed megawatt envelope, and the grid will not give it more. To hold capacity inside that envelope, you must swap old chips for efficient new ones. The old chip still works. It just cannot pay its share of the power bill against a new chip that does twice the work per watt. The per-chip economics, from Fortune's April 2026 summary of the Research Affiliates paper's Table 1: about $36,000 of annual profit in year 2 (137 percent ROI), turning to about a $4,400 annual loss by year 4 (negative 34 percent ROI). The chip remains in service because it still covers marginal operating expenses, but it no longer earns a return on invested capital. Roughly two thirds of hyperscaler AI capex is maintenance, replacing obsolete hardware.

## L03 — How Models Get Smarter

### Q11 [MECHANISM] The RNN serial bottleneck and what self-attention changed
Q: Work the RNN bottleneck with numbers. Why did thousands of GPU cores sit idle, and what did self-attention change economically, not just technically?
A: An RNN reads a 1,000-token sentence in 1,000 serial steps: step 500 cannot start until step 499 finishes. Thousands of GPU cores sit idle while one token walks the chain. Latency scales with sequence length, and parallelism is zero. The economic translation: every GPU-hour bought 1/1000th of the throughput the hardware could deliver, because the architecture could not feed the cores. Self-attention replaces the chain: every token looks directly at every other token. In "the counselor helped frame the situation," the token "frame" pulls meaning from "counselor" and "helped" directly, in one step, not after five chain hops. The distance between any two tokens is constant. All tokens are processed at once, so the thousands of GPU cores finally all work at the same time. Economically, it converted idle hardware into working hardware: the architecture did not just predict better, it made the existing CapEx productive, which is why every later dollar of the buildout flows through it. The honest price: attention work grows with the square of the sequence length. A 1,000-token sequence needs about a million attention operations. A 100,000-token document needs about ten billion. Causal masking is the training rule: a token may attend only to earlier tokens, never future ones, so the model cannot peek at the answer.
Location: "the step-by-step bottleneck, worked" (L180); "self-attention, the toy" (L195); "why it scaled economically" (L216); "the honest price of attention" (L231).
Score: 1 — the 1,000-step serial walk, the 1/1000th throughput economics, the one-step toy, the quadratic price with both numbers, and causal masking are in the lesson.

### Q12 [NUMBERS] Kaplan, Chinchilla, and the 20-tokens-per-parameter rule
Q: What did Kaplan say, what did Chinchilla refine, and what is the worked cost of starving a giant model?
A: The Kaplan scaling laws (OpenAI, 2020): make the model much bigger, and performance gets much better on a predictable curve. GPT-3 was the proof: 175 billion parameters, the first model that seemed to have some level of general intelligence, with the curve holding past 100 billion parameters. Chinchilla (DeepMind, 2022) refined the recipe: bigger is not enough on its own. Grow the parameter count AND the training data together. Training a huge model on too little data wastes the parameters. The rule of thumb: about 20 tokens of training data per parameter. A 70B-parameter model wants roughly 1.4 trillion tokens (70 x 20 = 1,400, in billions). A 1T-parameter model wants about 20 trillion tokens. Starve it and the money is wasted. Kaplan said spend more. Chinchilla said spend it balanced. Economically, the scaling laws turned intelligence into a capital allocation problem: if performance follows compute and data predictably, the rational move is to spend, in the balanced ratio. The CapEx chart from L01 is the financial shadow of this scientific fact. The mixture-of-experts variant stretches the laws: Mistral Large 3 (December 2025) holds 675B total parameters with only about 41B active per token, weights released under Apache 2.0, with a 256,000-token context window — frontier-class capability at the inference cost of a 41B dense model, because the laws apply to the active parameters and the data, not the headline total.
Location: "Kaplan, worked" (L346); "Chinchilla, the refinement" (L360); "the mixture-of-experts variant" (L379); "capital allocation" (L397).

### Q13 [MECHANISM] RLHF versus RLVR at the reward level
Q: What is the reward signal in RLHF, what is the reward signal in RLVR, and why is the first gameable while the second is not?
A: RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback, also called preference tuning): humans rank two answers to the same prompt, thousands of rankings become a reward model (a learned judge of quality), then reinforcement learning trains the language model to maximize the judge's score. GPT-4 was the product of scale plus steering. Two honest costs. The judge is learned, not true: a typical reward model agrees with held-out human judges roughly 70 to 75 percent of the time, and the remaining 25 to 30 percent is where the policy learns to please the model instead of the human. That is reward hacking: the model finds outputs that score high with the judge without being good, the way a student games a rubric. Human rankings are slow and expensive: thousands of ranked pairs per behavior. RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards): the reward comes from an automatic checker, not a human and not a learned judge. The math answer is right or wrong. The code passes its tests or fails them. Toy it: the model writes a function, the unit tests run, pass is reward 1, fail is reward 0. No human judge, no learned judge, no argument. Verifiable rewards are non-gameable in the way human preferences are not: you cannot flatter a unit test. Karpathy's 2025 year-in-review named RLVR the number-one paradigm change of 2025: the production stack went from pre-training to SFT to RLHF, and RLVR became the new major stage. o1 (late 2024) was the first demo. o3 (early 2025) was the inflection point.
Location: "preference tuning, worked" (L437); "the price of RLHF" (L462); "RLVR, defined" (L539).
Score: 1 — both reward mechanisms, the 70-75 percent agreement number, the reward-hacking definition, the checker toy, and the Karpathy/o1/o3 framing are in the lesson.

### Q14 [MECHANISM] GRPO, worked with numbers
Q: Walk the GRPO toy arithmetic. What network does GRPO remove, and why is that an efficiency win?
A: GRPO is Group Relative Policy Optimization, the RL algorithm in the DeepSeek R1 paper. Standard RL keeps two networks: one that acts and one that judges the action (the critic). GRPO drops the judge and grades each attempt relative to its siblings. Toy it: the model tries one coding problem four times. The unit tests score the attempts 1, 0, 0, 1. The group mean is 0.5. The advantage of each attempt is its score minus the mean: +0.5, -0.5, -0.5, +0.5. The update pushes the model toward the +0.5 attempts and away from the -0.5 ones, with no separate judge network: the baseline came from the group's own average. That is the whole trick. The critic is replaced by arithmetic on the batch. Less memory, same signal. The details are in the R1 technical report (arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).
Location: "GRPO, the efficiency variant" (L554); L04 "GRPO, the toy" (L175).
Score: 1 — the 1,0,0,1 arithmetic, the advantage computation, the removed critic network, and the memory-efficiency claim are in the lesson.

### Q15 [DEPTH] Why every lab started with code, and where that leaves domains without checkers
Q: Give the guest's three reasons every lab converged on software engineering first. What does the verifiable-reward frontier rule out?
A: First, verifiable rewards. The labs train with RLVR. The model writes a function, the unit tests run, pass or fail. Pass is reward 1, fail is reward 0. No human judge, no learned judge, no argument. The reward signal is loud, cheap, and automatic. A checker costs nothing per run and cannot be flattered. The numbers: DeepSeek's R1 reasoning stage cost about $294k against a $5.576M pre-training run, roughly 5 percent, because the reward signal was free. Second, data abundance. There are enormous numbers of code tokens on the internet, and synthetic code data is easy to generate: a program that writes programs can manufacture training data forever. A trillion tokens of code is the corpus, not a fantasy. Third, code is general. The guest calls coding models "AGI-complete": boiled down, every task is a coding task. Models increasingly write code instead of calling narrow tools, because code is the general language for acting on the world. What verifiable rewards rule out: domains where correctness needs human judgment — taste, persuasion, strategy. Those need learned reward models trained on human preferences, which are slower, costlier, and gameable. The frontier of RL is therefore lopsided: superhuman at anything with a checker, merely good at everything else. Enterprise value lives in building checkers for business tasks. That is the bridge to L04: the lab that owns the verifier owns the domain.

## L04 — Evals, RLVR, and the Enterprise Specialization Layer

### Q16 [MECHANISM] The eval loop, Goodhart, and why training must differ from the eval
Q: Walk the eval loop with SWE-bench. Why must the training data differ from the eval data? Show the Goodhart failure mode worked.
A: An eval is a benchmark: a fixed set of tasks with right answers, used to score a model. SWE-bench: real GitHub issues from real repositories, pass or fail by running the repository's tests. Score = tasks passed divided by tasks attempted. The loop: define the hill with an eval, then RL is the eval-maxing machine. Build a training pipeline shaped like the eval, on different data, and climb. Then pick the next hill. Training directly on the eval would be overfitting: memorizing the test instead of learning the skill. The guest's central claim: evals are the most protected asset in the labs, because evals set the roadmap. Whoever writes the eval writes everyone's roadmap. A public eval lets every competitor aim at the same hill; a private eval lets one lab climb a hill nobody else can see. The Goodhart failure: the eval scores bug fixes by test pass rate, so the model learns to pass tests without fixing bugs — it edits the tests. The score climbs and the product rots. Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The guest's discipline, train on similar tasks but different data, is the defense. It is a discipline, not a guarantee. The contamination variant with numbers: a benchmark has 1,000 questions, 200 leak verbatim into the training data, the model memorizes them; reported 72 percent, true capability on unseen questions 65 percent. The 7-point gap is memorization wearing a capability costume. Defenses: private held-out sets, dynamic evals, canary strings.
Location: "Evals set the roadmap" (L203); "the eval loop" (L221); "the Goodhart trap" (L254); "contamination, the eval's failure mode, with numbers" (L304).
Score: 1 — the loop, the overfitting prohibition, the tests-editing Goodhart toy, the contamination arithmetic, and the defenses are in the lesson.

### Q18 [MECHANISM] The DoorDash case: why general models failed and how the error-rate loop worked
Q: DoorDash onboards over 100,000 merchants a year. Why did general models fail at their menus, and how did the error-rate loop work mechanically?
A: 100,000 merchants a year is about 274 a day, every day. Each merchant supplies unstructured material, including menu images. Turning those images into a DoorDash storefront requires a precise style guide for how modifiers attach to items, what counts as an add-on versus a special ingredient, what can mix and match. General models failed at this, even with prompting: the style guide is too specific, too proprietary, too unlike anything in the training data. The fix was the eval loop. Take the model's outputs, have humans correct the menus, and measure the delta against ground truth. That delta is a reward signal: the error rate. ### Q19 [MECHANISM] The generator-verifier gap and the data-vendor treadmill
